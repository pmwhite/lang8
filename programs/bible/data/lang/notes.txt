= greek-letters	Reading Greek	greek
Greek has 24 letters and is read from left to right. Modern editions of the New Testament print it in small letters, with a capital at the start of a paragraph, a quotation or a name: Ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος, “In the beginning was the Word” (John 1:1).
Many letters look and sound like English ones: α a, β b, δ d, ε e, ι i, κ k, λ l, μ m, ο o, π p, τ t. A few look like English letters but are not: η is a long e, not n; ν is n, not v; ρ is r, not p; χ is ch as in loch, not x; ω is a long o, not w. γ is g, but before γ, κ, χ or ξ it is said ng, as in ἄγγελος, “angel”.
Sigma has two shapes: σ inside a word and ς at the end, as in λόγος. Some pairs of vowels are said as one sound: αι, ει, οι, υι, αυ, ευ and ου.
A small iota written under a vowel, as in ἀρχῇ, is the **iota subscript**. Most readers do not pronounce it, but it matters for grammar, because it often marks the dative.
Greek is pronounced in more than one way today. The app’s **Alphabet** gives each letter’s sound in the way you choose: Erasmian, reconstructed Koine or modern Greek. Any of them serves for reading, if you keep to one.

= greek-breathings	Breathing Marks	greek
Every Greek word that begins with a vowel has a small mark over its first letter, called a **breathing**. The **smooth breathing** (᾿) has no sound: ἐν is said “en”. The **rough breathing** (῾) adds an h before the vowel: ἁμαρτία is said “hamartia”, and ἡ is said “hē”.
When a word begins with a pair of vowels, the breathing sits on the second: αὐτός, εἰμί, οὐ, υἱός. On a capital it stands just before the letter: Ἐν, Ἰησοῦς. A word that begins with ρ or υ always has the rough breathing: ῥῆμα, “word”; υἱός, “son”.
The breathing can be the only difference between two words, so it is worth noticing. ἐν means “in”, but ἕν means “one”: χωρὶς αὐτοῦ ἐγένετο οὐδὲ ἓν, “without him not one thing came into being” (John 1:3). οὐ means “not”, but οὗ means “where” or “of whom”. The rough breathing also helps tell the relative pronoun (ὅς, ἥ, ὅ) from other words.

= greek-accents	Accents	greek
Most Greek words carry one accent mark: the acute (ά), the grave (ὰ) or the circumflex (ᾶ). It shows which syllable is stressed. The earliest manuscripts were written without accents; editors added them later to help readers.
A reader can mostly read past them. Stress the marked syllable; the three marks rarely need telling apart. An acute on the last syllable is simply written grave when another word follows, so θεός appears as θεὸς ἦν in John 1:1.
Some short words have no accent of their own and lean forward on the next word: the article ὁ, ἡ, οἱ, αἱ, and words such as ἐν, εἰς, ἐκ, οὐ and εἰ. Others, such as μου, σου, ἐστιν and τις, lean back on the word before and may give it a second accent: τὸ ὄνομά σου, “your name” (Matthew 6:9).
Now and then a mark is the only difference between two words, and then it matters: εἰ, “if”, and εἶ, “you are”; τίς, “who?”, and τις, “someone”; ἤ, “or”, and ἡ, “the”, which differ in breathing and accent. In τὸν νόμον ἢ τοὺς προφήτας, “the Law or the Prophets” (Matthew 5:17), ἢ is “or”. A circumflex on an ending is often the sign of a contract verb, as in ἀγαπῶ.

= greek-punctuation	Punctuation and Questions	greek
Greek editions use the comma (,) and the full stop (.) as English does. Two marks differ. A raised dot (·) is a pause like a colon or semicolon, and often comes before quoted words: λέγει αὐτῷ ὁ Ἰησοῦς· ἐγώ εἰμι ἡ ὁδός, “Jesus says to him, ‘I am the way’” (John 14:6). A mark like the English semicolon (;) is the Greek question mark: πιστεύεις τοῦτο; “Do you believe this?” (John 11:26).
Greek has no quotation marks. A capital letter, the raised dot or the word ὅτι often shows where speech begins. A mark like an apostrophe shows that a vowel has been dropped before another vowel: δι᾽ αὐτοῦ for διὰ αὐτοῦ, “through him” (John 1:3); ἀλλ᾽ for ἀλλά, “but”.
The punctuation is the editors’, not the writers’, and editions sometimes differ.
Questions often begin with τίς, “who? which?”, or τί, “what? why?”: τί γὰρ ἡ γραφὴ λέγει; “For what does the Scripture say?” (Romans 4:3). Without its accent, τις means “someone” or “a certain”, and τι “something”: ἐὰν μή τις γεννηθῇ ἄνωθεν, “unless someone is born again” (John 3:3). A question with οὐ expects the answer yes; one with μή expects no.

= greek-article	The Article	greek
ὁ, ἡ, τό is the Greek word for “the”. It changes form to match its noun in gender, case and number, so it is one of the best clues to what a noun is doing: in τὸν λόγον the article alone shows a masculine accusative singular. Learn this table early; the endings of most nouns echo it.
| Case | Masculine | Feminine | Neuter |
| Nominative singular | ὁ | ἡ | τό |
| Genitive singular | τοῦ | τῆς | τοῦ |
| Dative singular | τῷ | τῇ | τῷ |
| Accusative singular | τόν | τήν | τό |
| Nominative plural | οἱ | αἱ | τά |
| Genitive plural | τῶν | τῶν | τῶν |
| Dative plural | τοῖς | ταῖς | τοῖς |
| Accusative plural | τούς | τάς | τά |
Greek has no word for “a”. ἄνθρωπος can mean “a man” or simply “man”; the setting decides.
Greek uses the article where English does not. Names often have it: ὁ Ἰησοῦς, “Jesus”. So can words for ideas: ἡ ἀγάπη, “love” (1 Corinthians 13:13). And it can make almost any word into a noun: ὁ πιστεύων is “the one who believes” (John 3:16), and τὸ ζῆν “living” (Philippians 1:21). With δέ it can stand for people already named: οἱ δὲ εἶπαν, “and they said” (Acts 16:31).
Before another word, τόν, τήν and τό are written with a grave accent: τὸν θεόν. ὁ, ἡ, οἱ and αἱ have no accent; the relative pronoun ὅ, ἥ, οἵ, αἵ has one.

= greek-cases	The Cases	greek
English shows what a noun is doing mostly by word order: in “the dog bit the man”, the first noun did the biting. Greek shows it by the word’s ending. Each set of endings belongs to a **case**, and the case tells the word’s job in the sentence. Because the ending carries the meaning, Greek can put its words in almost any order.
| Case | Its usual job | Example |
| Nominative | the subject | ὁ λόγος, the Word |
| Genitive | of: belonging, source, description | τοῦ θεοῦ, of God |
| Dative | to or for; by means of; in | αὐτῷ, to him |
| Accusative | the object | τὸν κόσμον, the world |
| Vocative | someone spoken to | κύριε, Lord |
To find a case, look at the ending and at the article, which often shows the case more plainly than the noun. Some endings are shared, as τά is nominative or accusative, and then the sentence decides.
A few words, mostly Hebrew names such as Ἀβραάμ, are **indeclinable**: their ending never changes, and only the article or the sentence shows the case. In ἐπίστευσεν δὲ Ἀβραὰμ τῷ θεῷ, “Abraham believed God” (Romans 4:3), Ἀβραάμ is the subject.

= greek-nominative	The Nominative	greek
The nominative is the case of the **subject**, the one who does the action or is described: ἠγάπησεν ὁ θεὸς τὸν κόσμον, “God loved the world” (John 3:16). ὁ θεός is nominative, though it comes after the verb.
It is also the case of a word that describes or renames the subject after verbs such as εἰμί, “I am”, and γίνομαι, “I become”: ὁ λόγος σὰρξ ἐγένετο, “the Word became flesh” (John 1:14). Both ὁ λόγος and σάρξ are nominative. When two nominatives stand with such a verb, the one with the article is usually the subject.
A noun’s dictionary form is its nominative singular: λόγος, ἀρχή, σάρξ. Common nominative endings are -ος, -η, -α and -ον in the singular, and -οι, -αι and -α in the plural; third declension nouns vary more.
If a sentence has no nominative, the subject is in the verb: πιστεύεις τοῦτο; “Do you believe this?” (John 11:26).

= greek-genitive	The Genitive	greek
The genitive most often answers “of what?” or “whose?”, and is usually translated with **of** or an English possessive: ὁ ἀμνὸς τοῦ θεοῦ, “the Lamb of God” (John 1:29); τὸ ὄνομά σου, “your name” (Matthew 6:9). It normally follows the noun it describes.
It can show belonging, family, a quality, a source, or the whole that something is part of. Sometimes “of” leaves a choice to the reader: ἡ ἀγάπη τοῦ θεοῦ (1 John 5:3) could be God’s love or love for God, and the setting decides.
It has other uses too. Many prepositions take it, such as ἐκ, “out of”, ἀπό, “from”, and διά, “through”: διὰ πίστεως, “through faith” (Ephesians 2:8). After a comparative it means “than”: μείζων δὲ τούτων, “greater than these” (1 Corinthians 13:13). Some verbs take their object in the genitive: τὰ πρόβατα τῆς φωνῆς αὐτοῦ ἀκούει, “the sheep hear his voice” (John 10:3). And a participle and noun in the genitive can make a clause of their own, the genitive absolute.
Genitive endings include -ου, -ης and -ας, and in the third declension -ος. Every genitive plural ends in -ων.

= greek-dative	The Dative	greek
The dative is the case of the **indirect object**, the one to or for whom something is done: λέγει αὐτῷ, “he says to him” (John 14:6); δὸς ἡμῖν σήμερον, “give us today” (Matthew 6:11).
It also shows the **means** by which something is done, often translated “by” or “with”: τῇ γὰρ χάριτί ἐστε σεσῳσμένοι, “for by grace you have been saved” (Ephesians 2:8). And it can mark a place or a time: τῇ ἐπαύριον, “on the next day” (John 1:29).
Some verbs take their object in the dative, among them πιστεύω, “believe”, and ἀκολουθέω, “follow”: ἐπίστευσεν δὲ Ἀβραὰμ τῷ θεῷ, “Abraham believed God” (Romans 4:3). The preposition ἐν always takes the dative: ἐν ἀρχῇ, “in the beginning” (John 1:1).
Dative endings usually have an iota, often written small under the vowel: -ῳ, -ῃ, -ᾳ and -ι in the singular, and -οις, -αις and -σι(ν) in the plural.

= greek-accusative	The Accusative	greek
The accusative is the case of the **direct object**, what or whom the action is done to: ἠγάπησεν ὁ θεὸς τὸν κόσμον, “God loved the world” (John 3:16). In English the object is known by its place after the verb; in Greek it is known by its ending, wherever it stands: τὴν ψυχὴν αὐτοῦ τίθησιν, “he lays down his life” (John 10:11).
After prepositions the accusative often shows movement toward or into: εἰς αὐτόν, “in him”, literally “into him” (John 3:16); πρὸς τὸν θεόν, “with God”, literally “toward God” (John 1:1). It can also show how long something lasts, and it is the case of the subject of an infinitive.
Common endings are -ον, -ην, -αν and -α in the singular, and -ους, -ας and -α in the plural. A neuter noun’s accusative is always the same as its nominative, so the sentence shows which it is.

= greek-vocative	The Vocative	greek
The vocative is the case of someone spoken to directly, as in “Lord, help me”. English marks it with a comma, and older English with “O”.
Greek often uses the nominative form for this, but some nouns have a vocative of their own. Second declension nouns in -ος end in -ε: κύριε, “Lord”, from κύριος, as in ἔρχου, κύριε Ἰησοῦ, “Come, Lord Jesus” (Revelation 22:20). Some third declension nouns use a short form: πάτερ ἡμῶν, “our Father” (Matthew 6:9), from πατήρ. In the plural the vocative is always the same as the nominative: ἀδελφοί, “brothers” (Romans 12:1).
Sometimes ὦ, “O”, comes before it: ὦ ἄνθρωπε, “O man” (Romans 2:1). And a plain nominative can be used to address someone: Ἰωσὴφ υἱὸς Δαυίδ, “Joseph, son of David” (Matthew 1:20).

= greek-gender-number	Gender and Number	greek
Every Greek noun has a **gender**: masculine, feminine or neuter. It belongs to the word, not always to the thing: λόγος, “word”, is masculine; ἀγάπη, “love”, is feminine; παιδίον, “child”, is neuter. Words for men are usually masculine and words for women feminine, but for most nouns the gender is learned with the word. Lexicons give it with the article: ὁ λόγος, ἡ ἀγάπη, τὸ παιδίον.
Gender matters because the words that go with a noun, its article, adjectives and pronouns, take the same gender. That is how a reader sees which words belong together.
**Number** is singular, for one, or plural, for more than one; New Testament Greek has no dual. A neuter plural subject often takes a singular verb: τὰ πρόβατα … ἀκούει, “the sheep hear” (John 10:3), where ἀκούει is singular.
Each noun follows one of three patterns of endings, called **declensions**.

= greek-first-declension	First Declension Nouns	greek
The first declension holds mostly feminine nouns ending in -η or -α, such as ἀρχή, “beginning”, ἡμέρα, “day”, and δόξα, “glory”. Their endings follow the feminine article closely.
| Case | Singular | Plural |
| Nominative | ἀρχή | ἀρχαί |
| Genitive | ἀρχῆς | ἀρχῶν |
| Dative | ἀρχῇ | ἀρχαῖς |
| Accusative | ἀρχήν | ἀρχάς |
Nouns whose stem ends in ε, ι or ρ keep α through the singular: ἡμέρα, ἡμέρας, ἡμέρᾳ, ἡμέραν. Then -ας can be genitive singular or accusative plural, and the article tells them apart: τῆς ἡμέρας, “of the day”; τὰς ἡμέρας, “the days”. Nouns like δόξα have α in the nominative and accusative but η in the genitive and dative: δόξης, δόξῃ. The plural is the same for all of them.
A few first declension nouns are masculine, such as προφήτης, “prophet”, and μαθητής, “disciple”. They take the masculine article and have the genitive in -ου: τοῦ προφήτου.

= greek-second-declension	Second Declension Nouns	greek
The second declension holds most masculine nouns in -ος, such as λόγος, “word”, and most neuter nouns in -ον, such as ἔργον, “work”. Its endings are close to those of the masculine and neuter article.
| Case | λόγος, singular | λόγος, plural | ἔργον, singular | ἔργον, plural |
| Nominative | λόγος | λόγοι | ἔργον | ἔργα |
| Genitive | λόγου | λόγων | ἔργου | ἔργων |
| Dative | λόγῳ | λόγοις | ἔργῳ | ἔργοις |
| Accusative | λόγον | λόγους | ἔργον | ἔργα |
| Vocative | λόγε | λόγοι | ἔργον | ἔργα |
Two rules hold for every neuter noun, in any declension: the nominative and accusative are the same, and in the plural both end in -α. So ἔργα, “works”, may be the subject or the object.
A few nouns in -ος are feminine and take the feminine article, such as ἡ ὁδός, “the way”: ἐγώ εἰμι ἡ ὁδός, “I am the way” (John 14:6). There the article, not the ending, shows the gender.

= greek-third-declension	Third Declension Nouns	greek
The third declension has many patterns, but they share one habit: the noun’s **stem** shows plainly only from the genitive on. σάρξ, “flesh”, has the genitive σαρκός, so its stem is σαρκ-; the ξ of σάρξ is κ and σ written together. Lexicons give each noun with its genitive, as σάρξ, σαρκός, ἡ, and it is worth learning the two together.
| Case | σάρξ, singular | σάρξ, plural | πνεῦμα, singular | πνεῦμα, plural |
| Nominative | σάρξ | σάρκες | πνεῦμα | πνεύματα |
| Genitive | σαρκός | σαρκῶν | πνεύματος | πνευμάτων |
| Dative | σαρκί | σαρξί(ν) | πνεύματι | πνεύμασι(ν) |
| Accusative | σάρκα | σάρκας | πνεῦμα | πνεύματα |
The usual endings are -ος in the genitive singular, -ι in the dative and -α in the accusative, and -ες, -ων, -σι(ν) and -ας in the plural. Many common words follow them: χάρις, χάριτος, “grace”; πατήρ, πατρός, “father”; ὄνομα, ὀνόματος, “name”. Some have other genitives: πίστις, “faith”, has πίστεως, as in διὰ πίστεως, “through faith” (Ephesians 2:8).
Neuter nouns in -μα, such as πνεῦμα, “spirit”, and ὄνομα, are common and regular.

= greek-adjectives	Adjectives and Agreement	greek
An adjective describes a noun, and in Greek it takes the same gender, case and number as its noun. This is called **agreement**. The two endings may still look different, since the noun may follow another declension, but they agree.
| Case | Masculine | Feminine | Neuter |
| Nominative singular | ἀγαθός | ἀγαθή | ἀγαθόν |
| Genitive singular | ἀγαθοῦ | ἀγαθῆς | ἀγαθοῦ |
| Dative singular | ἀγαθῷ | ἀγαθῇ | ἀγαθῷ |
| Accusative singular | ἀγαθόν | ἀγαθήν | ἀγαθόν |
| Nominative plural | ἀγαθοί | ἀγαθαί | ἀγαθά |
| Genitive plural | ἀγαθῶν | ἀγαθῶν | ἀγαθῶν |
| Dative plural | ἀγαθοῖς | ἀγαθαῖς | ἀγαθοῖς |
| Accusative plural | ἀγαθούς | ἀγαθάς | ἀγαθά |
Where the adjective stands next to the article shows how it is used. If the article comes right before it, the adjective is **attributive** and describes the noun: ὁ ποιμὴν ὁ καλός, “the good shepherd” (John 10:11), which could also be written ὁ καλὸς ποιμήν. If the noun has the article and the adjective does not, the adjective is **predicate**, and English adds “is”: Μακάριοι οἱ πτωχοί, “Blessed are the poor” (Matthew 5:3). With its own article an adjective can stand for a noun, as οἱ πτωχοί, “the poor”, does there.
To compare, Greek uses forms such as μείζων, “greater”, or endings in -τερος, with the genitive for “than”: μείζων δὲ τούτων ἡ ἀγάπη, “but the greatest of these is love” (1 Corinthians 13:13). In New Testament Greek the comparative often does the work of the superlative, as it does there. Superlative forms in -τατος or -ιστος are less common: ἐλάχιστος, “least” (Matthew 5:19).

= greek-personal-pronouns	Personal Pronouns	greek
ἐγώ, “I”, and σύ, “you”, have these forms in the singular and plural:
| Case | I | we | you (one) | you (more than one) |
| Nominative | ἐγώ | ἡμεῖς | σύ | ὑμεῖς |
| Genitive | ἐμοῦ, μου | ἡμῶν | σοῦ, σου | ὑμῶν |
| Dative | ἐμοί, μοι | ἡμῖν | σοί, σοι | ὑμῖν |
| Accusative | ἐμέ, με | ἡμᾶς | σέ, σε | ὑμᾶς |
A Greek verb already shows its subject in its ending, so ἐγώ, “I”, and σύ, “you”, in the nominative add emphasis: ἐγώ εἰμι ἡ ὁδός, “I am the way” (John 14:6). In the other cases they are the ordinary words for “me”, “us” and “you”.
The short forms μου, μοι, με, σου, σοι and σε have no accent of their own and carry no emphasis. A genitive after a noun means “my”, “our” or “your”: πάτερ ἡμῶν, “our Father” (Matthew 6:9); τὸ ὄνομά σου, “your name”.
σύ is singular and ὑμεῖς plural. English “you” does not show the difference, but Greek always does.
For “he”, “she” and “they”, Greek uses αὐτός. Two other pronouns point back to people already named. ἑαυτοῦ, “himself, herself”, is the **reflexive pronoun**: παραδόντος ἑαυτόν, “who gave himself” (Galatians 2:20). ἀλλήλων, “one another”, is the **reciprocal pronoun**: ἵνα ἀγαπᾶτε ἀλλήλους, “that you love one another” (John 13:34).

= greek-autos	The Pronoun αὐτός	greek
αὐτός is the most common pronoun in the New Testament. It has three uses, and its place in the sentence shows which.
| Case | Masculine | Feminine | Neuter |
| Nominative singular | αὐτός | αὐτή | αὐτό |
| Genitive singular | αὐτοῦ | αὐτῆς | αὐτοῦ |
| Dative singular | αὐτῷ | αὐτῇ | αὐτῷ |
| Accusative singular | αὐτόν | αὐτήν | αὐτό |
| Nominative plural | αὐτοί | αὐταί | αὐτά |
| Genitive plural | αὐτῶν | αὐτῶν | αὐτῶν |
| Dative plural | αὐτοῖς | αὐταῖς | αὐτοῖς |
| Accusative plural | αὐτούς | αὐτάς | αὐτά |
Most often it means **he, she, it** or **they**: πᾶς ὁ πιστεύων εἰς αὐτόν, “everyone who believes in him” (John 3:16). It takes the gender of the noun it stands for, so αὐτή can mean “it” when that noun is feminine. A genitive after a noun means “his”, “her”, “its” or “their”: τὸ ὄνομα αὐτοῦ, “his name” (Matthew 1:21).
Beside a noun that has the article, but outside the article’s group, it means **-self**: αὐτὸς ὁ θεός, “God himself” (Revelation 21:3). Alone in the nominative it is an emphatic “he”: αὐτὸς γὰρ σώσει τὸν λαὸν αὐτοῦ, “for he will save his people” (Matthew 1:21).
Right after the article it means **the same**: τὸ δὲ αὐτὸ πνεῦμα, “but the same Spirit” (1 Corinthians 12:4).
Do not read αὐτή and αὐταί as αὕτη and αὗται, “this” and “these”: the breathing and the accent differ.

= greek-demonstratives	Demonstratives	greek
οὗτος means **this**, plural “these”, and ἐκεῖνος means **that**, plural “those”. They point to something near or far, in the text as well as in place or time.
| Case | Masculine | Feminine | Neuter |
| Nominative singular | οὗτος | αὕτη | τοῦτο |
| Genitive singular | τούτου | ταύτης | τούτου |
| Dative singular | τούτῳ | ταύτῃ | τούτῳ |
| Accusative singular | τοῦτον | ταύτην | τοῦτο |
| Nominative plural | οὗτοι | αὗται | ταῦτα |
| Genitive plural | τούτων | τούτων | τούτων |
| Dative plural | τούτοις | ταύταις | τούτοις |
| Accusative plural | τούτους | ταύτας | ταῦτα |
With a noun, they stand outside the article’s group, before or after it: ἐν ἐκείνῃ τῇ ἡμέρᾳ, “on that day” (John 14:20). Translate “this” or “that” with the noun; no “is” is added.
Alone, they work as pronouns: “this one”, “he”, “these things”. οὗτος ἦν ἐν ἀρχῇ πρὸς τὸν θεόν, “He was in the beginning with God” (John 1:2); ταῦτα δὲ γέγραπται, “but these things are written” (John 20:31).
οὗτος is easy to miss because its first letters change: most forms begin with τ, but the masculine and feminine nominatives begin with a rough breathing. Read the breathing carefully: αὕτη is “this” (feminine), but αὐτή is “she” or “same”. αὕτη δέ ἐστιν ἡ αἰώνιος ζωή, “and this is eternal life” (John 17:3). ἐκεῖνος has the endings of ἀγαθός, except the neuter ἐκεῖνο.

= greek-relative	The Relative Pronoun	greek
The relative pronoun ὅς, ἥ, ὅ means **who, which** or **that**. It begins a clause describing a noun already mentioned, called its **antecedent**, as in English “the word that you hear”.
It takes its gender and number from the antecedent, but its case from its own job in its clause: ὁ λόγος ὃν ἀκούετε, “the word that you hear” (John 14:24). λόγος is nominative, but ὃν is accusative, because it is the object of ἀκούετε, “you hear”.
| Case | Masculine | Feminine | Neuter |
| Nominative singular | ὅς | ἥ | ὅ |
| Genitive singular | οὗ | ἧς | οὗ |
| Dative singular | ᾧ | ᾗ | ᾧ |
| Accusative singular | ὅν | ἥν | ὅ |
| Nominative plural | οἵ | αἵ | ἅ |
| Genitive plural | ὧν | ὧν | ὧν |
| Dative plural | οἷς | αἷς | οἷς |
| Accusative plural | οὕς | ἅς | ἅ |
Its forms look like the article with a rough breathing and an accent. ὁ is “the”, but ὅ (or ὃ) is “which”; ἡ is “the”, but ἥ is “who”; οἱ and αἱ are “the”, but οἵ and αἵ “who”.
After a preposition it means “in which”, “through whom” and so on: ἐν ᾧ, “in which”. With ἄν or ἐάν it means “whoever”: ὃς ἐὰν οὖν λύσῃ, “whoever then breaks” (Matthew 5:19). ὅστις, “whoever, who”, works in much the same way.

= greek-prepositions	Prepositions	greek
A preposition is a small word such as “in”, “into” or “through”. In Greek it is followed by a noun in a particular case, and some prepositions change their meaning with the case: διὰ πίστεως is “through faith” (Ephesians 2:8), but διά with the accusative means “because of”.
| Preposition | With the genitive | With the dative | With the accusative |
| ἐν | — | in, among, by | — |
| εἰς | — | — | into, to, for |
| ἐκ, ἐξ | out of, from | — | — |
| ἀπό | from, away from | — | — |
| διά | through | — | because of |
| πρός | — | — | to, toward, with |
| ἐπί | on, over | on, at, on the basis of | on, onto, against |
| κατά | against, down from | — | according to, throughout |
| μετά | with | — | after |
| ὑπέρ | for, on behalf of | — | above, beyond |
| ὑπό | by (the one who acts) | — | under |
A preposition’s last vowel is often dropped before a vowel, with a mark like an apostrophe: δι᾽ αὐτοῦ, “through him” (John 1:3); μετ᾽ αὐτῶν, “with them” (Revelation 21:3). ἐκ becomes ἐξ before a vowel: ἐξ ὑμῶν, “from yourselves” (Ephesians 2:8). Before a rough breathing the consonant may change too: ἐφ᾽ for ἐπί, καθ᾽ for κατά.
Other common prepositions are σύν, “with” (dative); παρά, “from” (genitive), “beside” (dative), “alongside” (accusative); and περί, “about” (genitive), “around” (accusative). Prepositions also join the front of verbs: ἐκ and βάλλω, “throw”, make ἐκβάλλω, “throw out”.

= greek-verbs	Verbs: Person and Number	greek
A Greek verb carries a great deal in one word. Its ending shows the **person** of its subject (I, you, or he, she, it) and its **number** (one or more), so πιστεύω alone means “I believe” and πιστεύομεν “we believe”. A separate subject is needed only for emphasis or when the subject is named.
| Person | Singular | Plural |
| 1st | πιστεύω, I believe | πιστεύομεν, we believe |
| 2nd | πιστεύεις, you believe | πιστεύετε, you believe |
| 3rd | πιστεύει, he, she or it believes | πιστεύουσι(ν), they believe |
The ν in brackets comes and goes, often before a vowel; it is called the **movable nu** and changes nothing.
The dictionary lists a verb by its first person singular present, so πιστεύω is the entry for “to believe”. To read a form, take off the ending to find the **stem**, then look at what has been added before and after it.
A full parsing names five things: tense, voice, mood, person and number. πιστεύεις is present tense, active voice, indicative mood, second person singular: “you believe”. Infinitives and participles have no person; participles have case, gender and number instead.

= greek-tense-aspect	Tense and Aspect	greek
In English, tense is mostly about time. A Greek tense says two things. The first is **aspect**: how the writer presents the action, as going on, as a whole, or as complete with a lasting result. The second, in the indicative only, is **time**: past, present or future.
| Tense | Aspect | Time, in the indicative | Example |
| Present | going on | present | πιστεύω, I believe, I am believing |
| Imperfect | going on | past | ἐπίστευον, I was believing |
| Future | mainly time | future | πιστεύσω, I will believe |
| Aorist | as a whole | usually past | ἐπίστευσα, I believed |
| Perfect | complete, result stands | present | πεπίστευκα, I have believed |
| Pluperfect | complete, result stood | past | πεπιστεύκεισαν, they had believed |
Outside the indicative, in commands, the subjunctive, infinitives and participles, the tenses show aspect, not time. An aorist command such as πίστευσον, “believe” (Acts 16:31), is no more in the past than a present one; it presents the act as a whole.
Aspect is the writer’s view of an action, not a measure of the action itself. An aorist can describe something long or repeated, and a present something brief. Read the tense as a hint, and let the setting show how much it means.

= greek-present	The Present Tense	greek
The present tense presents an action as **going on** or **repeated**. In the indicative this is usually in present time: οὐδεὶς ἔρχεται πρὸς τὸν πατέρα, “no one comes to the Father” (John 14:6). English may need “I believe”, “I am believing” or “I keep believing”; choose what fits the setting.
Greek stories often use the present for past events, to make a scene vivid. This is the **historical present**, and translations usually give it as a past: Τῇ ἐπαύριον βλέπει τὸν Ἰησοῦν, “The next day he saw Jesus” (John 1:29).
Outside the indicative the present shows aspect, not time. A present command often asks for something to go on or become a habit: μετανοεῖτε καὶ πιστεύετε, “repent and believe” (Mark 1:15).
The present active endings are -ω, -εις, -ει, -ομεν, -ετε, -ουσι(ν); the middle and passive endings are -ομαι, -ῃ, -εται, -ομεθα, -εσθε, -ονται. The imperfect is built on the same stem.

= greek-imperfect	The Imperfect and the Augment	greek
The imperfect presents an action as **going on or repeated in past time**: “was doing”, “used to do” or “began to do”. ἐδίδασκεν αὐτούς, “he began to teach them” (Matthew 5:2); Ἠγάπα δὲ ὁ Ἰησοῦς τὴν Μάρθαν, “Now Jesus loved Martha” (John 11:5).
It is built on the present stem with two marks of the past. In front is the **augment**: ε- before a consonant (λύω, ἔλυον), or a lengthened first vowel when the stem begins with one (ἀγαπάω, ἠγάπα). At the end are the past endings.
| Person | Singular | Plural |
| 1st | ἔλυον, I was loosing | ἐλύομεν |
| 2nd | ἔλυες | ἐλύετε |
| 3rd | ἔλυε(ν) | ἔλυον |
ἔλυον is either “I was loosing” or “they were loosing”; the setting shows which.
In a verb compounded with a preposition the augment comes after the preposition, so look inside the word: ἐκβάλλω has the imperfect ἐξέβαλλον.
The augment appears only in the indicative, and it also marks the aorist and the pluperfect. When a verb begins with ε- or a lengthened vowel, think of the past, then look at the stem: the present stem means imperfect, and another stem usually aorist. ἦν, “was”, is the imperfect of εἰμί.

= greek-future	The Future Tense	greek
The future presents an action as **happening in future time**: καὶ σωθήσῃ σύ, “and you will be saved” (Acts 16:31).
Its usual mark is σ between the stem and the ending: λύω, λύσω, “I will loose”; πιστεύω, πιστεύσω. The endings are those of the present: -σω, -σεις, -σει, -σομεν, -σετε, -σουσι(ν). The σ can hide when it meets a consonant: γράφω, γράψω (φ and σ make ψ); ἄγω, ἄξω (γ and σ make ξ). Contract verbs lengthen their vowel: ἀγαπάω, ἀγαπήσω. Verbs whose stem ends in λ, μ, ν or ρ have no σ and use the endings of a contract verb instead: μένω, μενῶ.
Many verbs have a future with middle endings but an active meaning: γνώσεσθε, “you will know” (John 14:20), from γινώσκω. The future passive has -θησ-, as σωθήσῃ does.
The future sometimes gives a command, as in the Law: ἀγαπήσεις τὸν πλησίον σου ὡς σεαυτόν, “You shall love your neighbour as yourself” (Matthew 22:39).

@see greek-voice	Voice

= greek-aorist	The Aorist Tense	greek
The aorist is the usual tense of narrative. It presents an action **as a whole**, without describing its progress; in the indicative this is usually in past time. οὕτως γὰρ ἠγάπησεν ὁ θεὸς τὸν κόσμον, “For God so loved the world” (John 3:16).
It is formed in two ways, with the same meaning. The **first aorist** adds σα to the stem, with the augment in front: λύω, ἔλυσα; ἀγαπάω, ἠγάπησα.
| Person | Singular | Plural |
| 1st | ἔλυσα, I loosed | ἐλύσαμεν |
| 2nd | ἔλυσας | ἐλύσατε |
| 3rd | ἔλυσε(ν) | ἔλυσαν |
The **second aorist** uses a different stem with the endings of the imperfect: λαμβάνω has ἔλαβον, as in ὅτε οὖν ἔλαβεν τὸ ὄξος, “when he had received the sour wine” (John 19:30). Others are εἶπον, “I said”, from λέγω; ἦλθον, “I came”, from ἔρχομαι; and εἶδον, “I saw”. These are learned one by one, and lexicons list them under the present.
The aorist passive has θη: ἠγέρθη, “he has risen”, literally “he was raised” (Matthew 28:6).
Outside the indicative there is no augment and no past time; only the aspect remains: πίστευσον, “believe” (Acts 16:31); ἵνα πιστεύσητε, “that you may believe” (John 20:31).

= greek-perfect	The Perfect and Pluperfect	greek
The perfect presents an action as **complete, with its result still standing**. τετέλεσται, “It is finished” (John 19:30): the work is done and stays done. γέγραπται, “it is written” (Matthew 4:4), is said of Scripture that was written and still stands.
Its mark is **reduplication**: the first consonant of the stem is repeated, with ε, in front of it. λύω has λέλυκα, “I have loosed”; πιστεύω has πεπίστευκα, “I have believed”; γράφω has γέγραφα: ὃ γέγραφα, γέγραφα, “What I have written, I have written” (John 19:22). The active usually has κ before the ending; the middle and passive do not: πεπλήρωται ὁ καιρός, “the time is fulfilled” (Mark 1:15). Verbs that begin with a vowel lengthen it instead.
| Person | Singular | Plural |
| 1st | λέλυκα, I have loosed | λελύκαμεν |
| 2nd | λέλυκας | λελύκατε |
| 3rd | λέλυκε(ν) | λελύκασι(ν) |
A few perfects are translated as presents. οἶδα means “I know”: Οἴδαμεν δὲ ὅτι, “And we know that” (Romans 8:28).
The **pluperfect** puts the completed action and its result in the past: “had done”. It is rare. It has endings with -ει-, and sometimes the augment as well: ᾔδει, “he knew” (John 6:6), from οἶδα.

= greek-voice	Voice: Active, Middle and Passive	greek
**Voice** shows how the subject is related to the action. In the **active** the subject does it: λύω, “I loose”. In the **passive** the subject receives it: λύομαι, “I am loosed”; ἐλογίσθη αὐτῷ εἰς δικαιοσύνην, “it was credited to him as righteousness” (Romans 4:3). The one who does the action is often named with ὑπό and the genitive, “by”.
In the **middle** the subject takes part in the action in some special way: acting for itself, on itself, or with a personal interest. ἀναστὰς βάπτισαι καὶ ἀπόλουσαι τὰς ἁμαρτίας σου, “Get up, be baptized and wash your sins away” (Acts 22:16), has two middle commands. English has no middle, so translations use active or passive words.
| Person | Active | Middle or passive |
| I | λύω | λύομαι |
| you (one) | λύεις | λύῃ |
| he, she, it | λύει | λύεται |
| we | λύομεν | λυόμεθα |
| you (more than one) | λύετε | λύεσθε |
| they | λύουσι(ν) | λύονται |
In the present, imperfect and perfect the middle and passive share their endings, so a parsing says **middle or passive** and the setting decides. In the future and aorist they differ: the aorist passive has θη (ἐλύθην), the aorist middle -σάμην (ἐλυσάμην).
Some verbs have only middle or passive forms but active meanings, such as ἔρχομαι, “I come”: ναὶ ἔρχομαι ταχύ, “Yes, I am coming soon” (Revelation 22:20). Older grammars call these **deponent** verbs; many now treat them as true middles. Either way, translate them as active. Their lexicon form ends in -ομαι.

= greek-indicative	Mood and the Indicative	greek
**Mood** shows how the speaker presents an action: as a fact, as something possible or wished for, or as a command. Greek has four moods: the indicative, the subjunctive, the optative and the imperative. Infinitives and participles are often listed with them, though they have no person.
The **indicative** states something as a fact or asks a plain question: Ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος, “In the beginning was the Word” (John 1:1); πιστεύεις τοῦτο; “Do you believe this?” (John 11:26). It is the only mood that shows time and the only one with the augment. It shows how a statement is put, not whether it is true.
After εἰ, “if”, the indicative presents a condition as real for the sake of the argument: “if, as is the case”. With a past tense, and ἄν in the other half of the sentence, it is a condition contrary to fact: “if he had…, he would have…”. With ἐάν, “if”, Greek uses the subjunctive.
Its negative is οὐ.

= greek-subjunctive	The Subjunctive	greek
The subjunctive speaks of what **may** happen: what is hoped for, intended or possible, rather than stated as fact. Its mark is a long connecting vowel, ω or η, where the indicative has ο or ε: λύομεν, “we loose”, but λύωμεν, “let us loose” or “that we may loose”.
| Person | Indicative | Subjunctive |
| 1st singular | λύω | λύω |
| 2nd singular | λύεις | λύῃς |
| 3rd singular | λύει | λύῃ |
| 1st plural | λύομεν | λύωμεν |
| 2nd plural | λύετε | λύητε |
| 3rd plural | λύουσι(ν) | λύωσι(ν) |
It is most often found after ἵνα, “so that”: ἵνα πᾶς ὁ πιστεύων εἰς αὐτὸν μὴ ἀπόληται ἀλλ᾽ ἔχῃ ζωὴν αἰώνιον, “that everyone who believes in him shall not perish but have eternal life” (John 3:16).
It follows ἐάν, “if”, and words such as ὅταν, “whenever”: ἐὰν μή τις γεννηθῇ ἄνωθεν, “unless someone is born again” (John 3:3). In the first person plural it means “let us”: προσερχώμεθα οὖν, “let us then approach” (Hebrews 4:16).
With μή it forbids: μὴ φοβηθῇς, “do not be afraid” (Matthew 1:20). With οὐ μή it is the strongest way to say something will not happen: οὐ μὴ περιπατήσῃ ἐν τῇ σκοτίᾳ, “will never walk in the darkness” (John 8:12).
The subjunctive is found mostly in the present and aorist, which differ in aspect, not time. It has no augment.

= greek-imperative	The Imperative	greek
The imperative gives a **command** or makes a request. Greek has second person imperatives, “(you) do this”, and also third person ones, “let him do this”, which English has to paraphrase.
| Person | Present | Aorist |
| 2nd singular | λῦε | λῦσον |
| 3rd singular | λυέτω | λυσάτω |
| 2nd plural | λύετε | λύσατε |
| 3rd plural | λυέτωσαν | λυσάτωσαν |
The present and aorist differ in aspect. The aorist presents the act as a whole: πίστευσον ἐπὶ τὸν κύριον Ἰησοῦν, “Believe in the Lord Jesus” (Acts 16:31). The present presents it as going on: μετανοεῖτε καὶ πιστεύετε, “repent and believe” (Mark 1:15).
In the Lord’s Prayer, ἁγιασθήτω τὸ ὄνομά σου, “hallowed be your name”, and ἐλθέτω ἡ βασιλεία σου, “your kingdom come”, are third person aorist imperatives (Matthew 6:9–10); δός, from δίδωμι, is “give” (Matthew 6:11).
The second person plural present imperative looks just like the indicative: πιστεύετε can be the command “believe” or the statement “you believe”. The setting decides.
To forbid, Greek uses μή with a present imperative, or μή with an aorist subjunctive: μὴ φοβηθῇς, “do not be afraid” (Matthew 1:20).

= greek-optative	The Optative	greek
The optative is rare in the New Testament, found fewer than seventy times. It expresses a **wish**, or a possibility put politely or at a distance. Its mark is οι or αι before the ending: λύοιμι, λύσαιμι, γένοιτο.
Most often it is a wish or prayer: γένοιτό μοι κατὰ τὸ ῥῆμά σου, “may it be to me according to your word” (Luke 1:38); Αὐτὸς δὲ ὁ κύριος τῆς εἰρήνης δῴη ὑμῖν τὴν εἰρήνην, “Now may the Lord of peace himself give you peace” (2 Thessalonians 3:16).
Paul often answers a wrong conclusion with μὴ γένοιτο, “by no means”, literally “may it not be” (Romans 6:2).
Luke also uses it in uncertain or polite questions, sometimes with ἄν: τί ἂν θέλοι ὁ σπερμολόγος οὗτος λέγειν; “What would this babbler want to say?” (Acts 17:18).
Its negative is μή.

@see greek-subjunctive	The subjunctive

= greek-infinitive	The Infinitive	greek
The infinitive is the verb used as a **noun**, like the English “to love”. It has tense and voice but no person, and it does not change for case, though an article in front of it can.
| Tense | Active | Middle | Passive |
| Present | λύειν | λύεσθαι | λύεσθαι |
| Aorist | λῦσαι | λύσασθαι | λυθῆναι |
| Second aorist | ἰδεῖν, from ὁράω | γενέσθαι, from γίνομαι | — |
| Perfect | λελυκέναι | λελύσθαι | λελύσθαι |
Its most common job is to complete another verb, such as δύναμαι, “I can”, θέλω, “I want”, or δεῖ, “it is necessary”: οὐ δύναται ἰδεῖν τὴν βασιλείαν τοῦ θεοῦ, “he cannot see the kingdom of God” (John 3:3). It can also give a purpose: οὐκ ἦλθον καταλῦσαι ἀλλὰ πληρῶσαι, “I have not come to abolish them but to fulfil them” (Matthew 5:17).
With the article it works as a noun: Ἐμοὶ γὰρ τὸ ζῆν Χριστὸς καὶ τὸ ἀποθανεῖν κέρδος, “For to me, to live is Christ and to die is gain” (Philippians 1:21). After a preposition it often makes a clause of time or reason: ἐν τῷ σπείρειν αὐτόν, “as he was sowing” (Matthew 13:4).
That last example shows that the subject of an infinitive, when it is given, is in the accusative: αὐτόν, “he”. As elsewhere outside the indicative, present and aorist infinitives differ in aspect, not time.

= greek-participle	The Participle	greek
A participle is a **verbal adjective**: a verb form that also behaves like an adjective, with case, gender and number. English words in -ing and -ed, such as “believing” and “written”, are participles. Greek uses them far more than English does.
| Tense | Active | Middle | Passive |
| Present | λύων | λυόμενος | λυόμενος |
| Aorist | λύσας | λυσάμενος | λυθείς |
| Perfect | λελυκώς | λελυμένος | λελυμένος |
Look for ντ in the active (λύοντος, πιστεύοντες) and μεν in the middle and passive (λυόμενος).
With the article, a participle works as an adjective or a noun: ὁ πιστεύων, “the one who believes” (John 3:16); ὁ αἴρων τὴν ἁμαρτίαν τοῦ κόσμου, “who takes away the sin of the world” (John 1:29).
Without the article, it usually adds to the main verb, telling when, why or how. Translate with “while”, “after”, “because”, “by”, or simply “and”: πορευθέντες οὖν μαθητεύσατε πάντα τὰ ἔθνη, βαπτίζοντες αὐτούς, “Go therefore and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them” (Matthew 28:19). A present participle usually describes action at the same time as the main verb, an aorist participle action before it.
A participle and a noun or pronoun in the genitive, standing apart from the rest of the sentence, make a **genitive absolute**, usually a clause of time: ἔτι ἁμαρτωλῶν ὄντων ἡμῶν, “while we were still sinners” (Romans 5:8); Τοῦ δὲ Ἰησοῦ γεννηθέντος, “after Jesus was born” (Matthew 2:1).

= greek-eimi	The Verb εἰμί	greek
εἰμί, “I am”, is irregular and very common, so it is worth learning by heart. It links a subject to a description, which is in the nominative, not the accusative: ὁ θεὸς ἀγάπη ἐστίν, “God is love” (1 John 4:8).
| Person | Present | Imperfect |
| 1st singular | εἰμί, I am | ἤμην, I was |
| 2nd singular | εἶ, you are | ἦς, you were |
| 3rd singular | ἐστί(ν), he, she or it is | ἦν, he, she or it was |
| 1st plural | ἐσμέν, we are | ἦμεν, we were |
| 2nd plural | ἐστέ, you are | ἦτε, you were |
| 3rd plural | εἰσί(ν), they are | ἦσαν, they were |
Most present forms lean on the word before them, so they often appear without an accent: ἐκ τοῦ θεοῦ ἐστιν, “is from God” (1 John 4:7). Do not confuse εἶ, “you are”, with εἰ, “if”. At the start of a sentence ἔστιν can mean “there is”.
Other forms are the future ἔσομαι, “I will be” (ἔσται, “he will be”, Revelation 21:3); the infinitive εἶναι, “to be”; and the participle ὤν, οὖσα, ὄν, “being”: ἔτι ἁμαρτωλῶν ὄντων ἡμῶν, “while we were still sinners” (Romans 5:8). εἰμί has no aorist; for “became” Greek uses γίνομαι: ὁ λόγος σὰρξ ἐγένετο (John 1:14).

= greek-contract-verbs	Contract Verbs	greek
Some verbs have a stem ending in a vowel, α, ε or ο: ἀγαπάω, “love”; ποιέω, “do, make”; πληρόω, “fulfil”. In the present and imperfect that vowel runs together, or **contracts**, with the ending, so the forms in the text look different from the lexicon form: ἀγαπάω appears as ἀγαπῶ, and ποιέω as ποιῶ. Lexicons list them uncontracted, so that the stem is plain.
| Person | ἀγαπάω | ποιέω | πληρόω |
| 1st singular | ἀγαπῶ | ποιῶ | πληρῶ |
| 2nd singular | ἀγαπᾷς | ποιεῖς | πληροῖς |
| 3rd singular | ἀγαπᾷ | ποιεῖ | πληροῖ |
| 1st plural | ἀγαπῶμεν | ποιοῦμεν | πληροῦμεν |
| 2nd plural | ἀγαπᾶτε | ποιεῖτε | πληροῦτε |
| 3rd plural | ἀγαπῶσι(ν) | ποιοῦσι(ν) | πληροῦσι(ν) |
A circumflex on the ending, as in ἀγαπῶμεν or ποιεῖ, is often the sign of a contract verb. Participles contract too: ὁ μὴ ἀγαπῶν, “whoever does not love” (1 John 4:8).
Outside the present and imperfect there is no contraction; the vowel lengthens before the tense’s mark instead: ἀγαπήσω, ἠγάπησα; ποιήσω; πληρώσω; πεπλήρωται, “is fulfilled” (Mark 1:15).

= greek-mi-verbs	Verbs in -μι	greek
A small group of very common verbs end in -μι rather than -ω in the lexicon: δίδωμι, “give”; τίθημι, “put, place”; ἵστημι, “stand”; ἀφίημι, “let go, forgive”; δείκνυμι, “show”. Their present has its own endings, and the present stem usually repeats the first consonant with ι: δι-δω-μι, τι-θη-μι.
| Person | δίδωμι | τίθημι |
| 1st singular | δίδωμι | τίθημι |
| 2nd singular | δίδως | τίθης |
| 3rd singular | δίδωσι(ν) | τίθησι(ν) |
| 1st plural | δίδομεν | τίθεμεν |
| 2nd plural | δίδοτε | τίθετε |
| 3rd plural | διδόασι(ν) | τιθέασι(ν) |
The key to reading them is the short stem underneath: δο or δω for δίδωμι, θε or θη for τίθημι, στα or στη for ἵστημι. Find it, and the verb is found: τὴν ψυχὴν αὐτοῦ τίθησιν, “he lays down his life” (John 10:11).
Their aorist often has κ where other verbs have σ: ἔδωκα, “I gave”; ἔθηκα, “I put”. τὸν υἱὸν τὸν μονογενῆ ἔδωκεν, “he gave his one and only Son” (John 3:16). Elsewhere in the aorist the bare stem shows: δός, “give” (Matthew 6:11).
Compounds are common, so look for the stem after a preposition: παρέδωκεν τὸ πνεῦμα, “he gave up his spirit” (John 19:30), and παραδόντος ἑαυτόν, “who gave himself” (Galatians 2:20), are from παραδίδωμι.

= greek-negation	Negation and Questions	greek
Greek has two words for “not”. **οὐ**, written οὐκ before a smooth breathing and οὐχ before a rough one, goes with the indicative: οὐ δύναται ἰδεῖν, “he cannot see” (John 3:3); οὐκ ἔγνω τὸν θεόν, “does not know God” (1 John 4:8).
**μή** goes with everything else: the subjunctive, the imperative, the infinitive and the participle. ὁ μὴ ἀγαπῶν, “whoever does not love” (1 John 4:8); ἵνα … μὴ ἀπόληται, “that he shall not perish” (John 3:16).
In a question, οὐ expects the answer yes: οὐχ οὗτός ἐστιν ὁ τοῦ τέκτονος υἱός; “Isn’t this the carpenter’s son?” (Matthew 13:55). μή, or μήτι, expects no, or a hesitant answer: μήτι οὗτός ἐστιν ὁ χριστός; “Could this be the Christ?” (John 4:29).
Two negatives together strengthen each other rather than cancel out. οὐ μή with the subjunctive means “never”, and οὐδενὶ οὐδὲν εἶπαν is “they said nothing to anyone” (Mark 16:8). Compound negatives begin the same way: οὐδείς and μηδείς, “no one”; οὐκέτι, “no longer”.

= greek-word-order	Word Order and Finding the Subject	greek
Greek endings show what each word is doing, so its word order is freer than English. Writers often put first what they want to stress. A reader therefore looks at endings, not positions.
A good order for working through a sentence:
1. Find the main verb, and from its ending its person and number.
2. Look for a nominative that agrees with it: that is the subject. If there is none, the subject is in the verb: “he”, “they”, “you”.
3. Look for an accusative: that is usually the object.
4. Group each article with its noun, and with the adjectives and genitives that belong to them.
5. Then fit in the rest: datives, prepositional phrases and participles.
John 3:16 runs verb, subject, object: ἠγάπησεν ὁ θεὸς τὸν κόσμον, “God loved the world”. In John 1:1, θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος, both nouns are nominative, and the article marks the subject: “the Word was God”.
Some small words never come first in their clause, but second: δέ, γάρ and οὖν. Translate them first: οὕτως γὰρ ἠγάπησεν ὁ θεός is “For God so loved”.

= greek-small-words	Small Joining Words	greek
These short words are among the most common in the New Testament, and they show how one thought leads to the next.
**καί** joins words and sentences: “and”. It can also mean “also” or “even”, and καί … καί is “both … and”.
**δέ** marks a new step: “and”, “but” or “now”, and often needs no English word. It never stands first in its clause: Ἠγάπα δὲ ὁ Ἰησοῦς, “Now Jesus loved” (John 11:5). μέν … δέ sets two things side by side: “on the one hand … on the other”.
**γάρ**, “for”, gives a reason or an explanation, and also stands second: οὕτως γὰρ ἠγάπησεν ὁ θεός, “For God so loved” (John 3:16).
**οὖν**, “therefore, so, then”, draws a conclusion or takes up a story again, and also stands second: Παρακαλῶ οὖν ὑμᾶς, “I urge you therefore” (Romans 12:1).
**ἵνα**, “so that, in order that”, gives a purpose, with the subjunctive (John 3:16). It can also give the content of something: αὕτη δέ ἐστιν ἡ αἰώνιος ζωὴ ἵνα γινώσκωσιν σέ, “And this is eternal life, that they know you” (John 17:3).
**ὅτι** means “that” after verbs of saying, knowing or believing, or “because”: ὅτι ὁ θεὸς ἀγάπη ἐστίν, “because God is love” (1 John 4:8). Before quoted words it works like an opening quotation mark and is not translated (Mark 1:15).
**ἀλλά**, “but”, marks a sharp contrast, and **ἰδού**, “look” or “behold”, calls attention to what follows: ἰδοὺ ἡ δούλη κυρίου, “Behold, the servant of the Lord” (Luke 1:38).

# ---- Hebrew ----

= hebrew-letters	Reading Hebrew	hebrew
Hebrew is written and read from **right to left**, and a Hebrew Bible opens at what an English reader would call the back. The alphabet has 22 letters, all consonants; there are no capitals. The vowels were added much later, as dots and dashes around the letters, by scribes called the Masoretes.
Five letters have a different shape at the end of a word:
| Name | Ordinary | Final |
| Kaf | כ | ך |
| Mem | מ | ם |
| Nun | נ | ן |
| Pe | פ | ף |
| Tsade | צ | ץ |
So מֶלֶךְ, “king”, ends in final kaf, and its plural מְלָכִים has the ordinary כ inside the word and final ם at the end.
Some letters are easily confused and are worth looking at closely: ב and כ; ד and ר; ה, ח and ת; ו, ז and final ן; ס and final ם. A dot can change a sound: בּ is b, but ב is v; שׁ, with its dot on the right, is sh, and שׂ, with its dot on the left, is s.
Four letters are said in the throat: א, ה, ח and ע. These **gutturals**, and often ר with them, cannot be doubled and prefer certain vowels, so words with them often look a little irregular.
The app’s **Alphabet** gives each letter’s sound, in the Israeli or the reconstructed Tiberian pronunciation.

= hebrew-vowels	The Vowel Points	hebrew
The vowels are small marks, called **points**, written mostly under the letters and sometimes above or beside them. Each vowel is said after the consonant it belongs to: בָּ is “ba”, not “ab”. So בְּרֵאשִׁית, “in the beginning” (Genesis 1:1), is read bə-rē-shit.
| Sign | Name | Sound |
| בָ | Qamets | a as in father |
| בַ | Patach | a as in father |
| בֵ | Tsere | e as in they |
| בֶ | Segol | e as in bet |
| בִ | Hireq | i as in machine |
| בֹ | Holem | o as in note |
| בוֹ | Holem vav | o as in note |
| בֻ | Qibbuts | oo as in food |
| בוּ | Shureq | oo as in food |
| בְ | Sheva | a very short a as in about, or silent |
| בֲ | Hatef patach | a short a |
Some letters also help spell a vowel: ו for o or u (וֹ, וּ), י after i or e (בִי, בֵי), and ה at the end of a word after a. The Hebrew Bible does not always use them, so a word may be spelled with or without them: הַגָּדוֹל and הַגָּדֹל are the same word, “the great”.
The sign ◌ָ is usually a, but in some unstressed closed syllables it is a short o, called **qamets hatuf**: כָּל־, “all”, is said kol (Genesis 1:31).
When a word ends in ח or ע, a patach under it is said before the letter: רוּחַ, “spirit, wind”, is ru-ach (Genesis 1:2). This is the **furtive patach**.

= hebrew-sheva	The Sheva	hebrew
The **sheva** (also spelled shewa), two dots one above the other under a letter (בְ), marks either a very short vowel or none at all. Which it is decides how a word is divided and said.
A sheva is **sounded**, like the a of “about”, at the start of a word, as in בְּרֵאשִׁית, bə-rē-shit; when it is the second of two shevas in a row in the middle of a word, as in יִשְׁמְעוּ, yish-mə-‘u, “they will understand” (Genesis 11:7); and under a letter doubled by a dagesh, as in הַמְּאֹרֹת, ham-mə-’o-rot, “the lights” (Genesis 1:16).
A sheva is **silent** when it closes a syllable after a short vowel, as the first sheva of יִשְׁמְעוּ does, and at the end of a word, as in מֶלֶךְ.
The gutturals א, ה, ח and ע do not take a plain sounded sheva. Instead they take a **composite sheva**, or hatef: a sheva joined to a short vowel. ◌ֲ is a short a, as in אֲנִי, “I”; ◌ֱ is a short e, as in אֱלֹהִים, “God”; ◌ֳ is a short o. These are always sounded.
The same sign serves for both kinds of sheva, and grammars differ over a few harder cases. For reading aloud, the rules above are enough.

@see hebrew-dagesh	The dagesh

= hebrew-dagesh	The Dagesh	hebrew
A **dagesh** is a dot inside a letter. It has two jobs, and both look the same.
In six letters, ב ג ד כ פ ת, a dagesh can mark the hard sound: בּ b, כּ k, פּ p, and in the Tiberian pronunciation גּ, דּ and תּ as well. This **dagesh lene** comes at the start of a word, or after a syllable that ends in a consonant: בְּרֵאשִׁית; מִדְבָּר, “wilderness”, mid-bar. Without it, ב is v, כ is kh, and פ is f.
In any letter except the gutturals and ר, a dagesh can **double** the letter: הַשָּׁמַיִם, “the heavens”, is hash-sha-ma-yim (Genesis 1:1). This **dagesh forte** often shows that something has happened to the word: the article has been added (הַ), a nun has merged into the next letter (מִכֹּל, “from every”, for מִן כֹּל, Genesis 2:16), or the verb is in a stem such as the Piel (דִּבֶּר, “he spoke”).
In the six letters, tell them apart by looking before the letter. If a vowel comes right before it, the dagesh doubles it; if not, it is the lene. So the בּ in דִּבֶּר follows a vowel and is doubled: dib-ber.
A dot in a final ה is something else again: a **mappiq**, which shows that the ה is a consonant, as in the suffix “her” or “it”: לְעָבְדָהּ וּלְשָׁמְרָהּ, “to work it and keep it” (Genesis 2:15).

= hebrew-maqqef-accents	Maqqef, Accents and Other Marks	hebrew
A **maqqef** (־) is a short raised line that joins two or more words so that they are said as one, with a single stress: עַל־פְּנֵי, “over the face of” (Genesis 1:2); אֶת־הָאוֹר, “the light” (Genesis 1:4). It often joins a short word, such as a preposition or the object marker, to the next. Read each word on its own; the maqqef does not change the meaning.
The Hebrew Bible also has a full set of **accents**, small marks above and below the words, called **cantillation**. They guide the chanting of the text, mark the stressed syllable, and divide each verse into parts, much as punctuation does. A reader can leave most of them aside. Two are worth knowing: the **atnach** (◌֑), under the main pause in the middle of a verse, and the **sof pasuq** (׃), which ends the verse.
At a major pause a word sometimes has a longer vowel: הַמַּיִם, “the waters” (Genesis 1:7), is הַמָּיִם at the end of Genesis 1:2. This is a **pausal form**, and it means the same.
Other marks are not words either. A short vertical line under a vowel (◌ֽ) is a metheg, or on the last word of a verse the silluq; both mark stress. A tall line between words (׀) is a paseq, a short pause. A פ or ס between verses marks the end of a paragraph.
@see hebrew-letters	Reading Hebrew

= hebrew-roots	Roots and Patterns	hebrew
Most Hebrew words are built on a **root** of three consonants that carries a core meaning. Vowels, prefixes and suffixes around the root make the particular word, much as English sing, sang, song and singer share s-ng. The root מ־ל־ך has to do with reigning:
| Word | Meaning | What it is |
| מָלַךְ | he reigned | verb, Qal |
| מֶלֶךְ | king | noun |
| מַלְכַּת | queen of | noun, construct (1 Kings 10:1) |
| מַלְכוּת | kingdom | noun (Daniel 9:1) |
| הִמְלִיךְ | he made king | verb, Hiphil (1 Samuel 15:35) |
| הָמְלַךְ | he was made king | verb, Hophal (Daniel 9:1) |
To find a root, take off any prefixes (such as ו, ה, ב, כ, ל and מ) and endings, and look at the consonants left. Often three remain. If only two can be seen, a weak letter may have dropped out or changed: a נ that has merged often leaves a dagesh, a final ה may fall away, and a middle ו or י may vanish.
Lexicons list a verb under its simplest form, the Qal perfect, “he did”, and English glosses it with an infinitive: אָמַר, “to say”. Verbs with a middle ו are listed under another form, as קוּם, “to arise”. Nouns are listed as themselves: דָּבָר, “word”.
Knowing the patterns helps with words not met before. A מ in front of a root often makes a place or an instrument, as מִזְבֵּחַ, “altar”, from זָבַח, “to sacrifice”; ◌וּת at the end often makes an abstract noun, as מַלְכוּת, “kingdom”.

= hebrew-article	The Article	hebrew
Hebrew has a word for “the” but none for “a”. The **article** is a prefix, הַ, joined to the front of a word, and it usually puts a dagesh in the next letter, doubling it: שָׁמַיִם, “heavens”, but הַשָּׁמַיִם, “the heavens” (Genesis 1:1); מֶלֶךְ, “king”, but הַמֶּלֶךְ, “the king”.
The gutturals and ר cannot be doubled, so before them the article changes. Before א, ע and ר it is usually הָ: הָאָרֶץ, “the earth” (Genesis 1:1); הָעִיר, “the city” (Judges 14:18). Before ה and ח it usually stays הַ, without a dagesh: הַחֹשֶׁךְ, “the darkness” (Genesis 1:4). Sometimes, before a guttural with qamets, it is הֶ: מֵהֶעָרִים, “from the cities” (Judges 20:15).
After the prepositions בְּ, כְּ and לְ, the ה of the article drops out and its vowel moves to the preposition: בְּ with הַגָּן becomes בַּגָּן, “in the garden” (Genesis 3:8); לְ with הָאוֹר becomes לָאוֹר, “to the light”, in “God called the light day” (Genesis 1:5). So a preposition with an a vowel, and a dagesh after it, usually means “the” is there.
A noun is definite without the article when it is a name, when it has a pronoun suffix (“my king”), or when it is bound to a definite noun in a construct chain. Do not mistake the article for הֲ, which asks a question.

= hebrew-prefixed-prepositions	Prepositions Joined to the Word	hebrew
Three common prepositions are single letters joined to the front of the next word: **בְּ**, “in, with, by”; **כְּ**, “like, as”; and **לְ**, “to, for”. בְּרֵאשִׁית is בְּ with רֵאשִׁית, “in the beginning” (Genesis 1:1). Their usual vowel is sheva, but it changes with what follows.
Before another sheva they take hireq: לְ with פְּנֵי becomes לִפְנֵי, “before” (Genesis 6:11); כְּ with דְּמוּתֵנוּ becomes כִּדְמוּתֵנוּ, “after our likeness” (Genesis 1:26). Before the article they take its vowel and the ה drops out: בַּגָּן, “in the garden” (Genesis 3:8). With אֱלֹהִים they become בֵּאלֹהִים, כֵּאלֹהִים and לֵאלֹהִים: כֵּאלֹהִים, “like God” (Genesis 3:5).
**מִן**, “from”, can stand as a word of its own, often joined by a maqqef: הֲמִן־הָעֵץ, “from the tree?” (Genesis 3:11). More often it is joined to the next word as מִ, its נ merged into the next letter as a dagesh: מִכֹּל, “from every” (Genesis 2:16); מִפְּנֵי, “from the presence of” (Genesis 3:8). Before a guttural it is מֵ: מֵעַל, “above”, literally “from upon” (Genesis 1:7).
Hebrew adjectives have no form for “more”, so מִן also means “than”: עָרוּם מִכֹּל חַיַּת הַשָּׂדֶה, “more crafty than any beast of the field” (Genesis 3:1); מַה־מָּתוֹק מִדְּבַשׁ, “What is sweeter than honey?” (Judges 14:18).
These prepositions take pronoun suffixes too: לוֹ, “to him”; מִמֶּנּוּ, “from it”.

= hebrew-waw	The Conjunction וְ	hebrew
The word for “and” is the prefix **וְ**, joined to the front of the next word: וְאֵת הָאָרֶץ, “and the earth” (Genesis 1:1). It can also mean “but”, “so”, “then” or “now”. Hebrew uses it far more than English uses “and”, and translations often give it another word or leave it out.
Its vowel changes with what follows. Before ב, מ or פ, and before a sheva, it becomes וּ, said u: וּבֵין, “and between” (Genesis 1:4); פְּרוּ וּרְבוּ וּמִלְאוּ, “Be fruitful and multiply and fill” (Genesis 1:22).
Before יְ the two run together as וִי: וִיהִי, “and let it be” (Genesis 1:6). Before a composite sheva it takes the matching short vowel, and before אֱלֹהִים and its forms it is וֵ: וֵאלֹהֵי יִצְחָק, “and the God of Isaac” (Genesis 28:13).
Often, before a stressed syllable, especially in pairs of words, it is וָ: תֹהוּ וָבֹהוּ, “formless and void” (Genesis 1:2); טוֹב וָרָע, “good and evil” (Genesis 2:17).
A וַ with a dagesh in the next letter, on a verb, is something more: the form that tells a story in the past, as וַיֹּאמֶר, “and he said”.

= hebrew-gender-number	Gender and Number	hebrew
Every Hebrew noun is **masculine** or **feminine**; there is no neuter. Feminine nouns often end in ◌ָה or ◌ת, but many do not, and the gender belongs to the word, not always to the thing.
Nouns have three numbers: singular, plural and dual. The usual endings are:
| | Masculine | Feminine |
| Singular | סוּס, horse | תּוֹרָה, law |
| Plural | סוּסִים, horses | תּוֹרוֹת, laws |
| Dual | יוֹמַיִם, two days | שְׁנָתַיִם, two years |
The **dual**, in ◌ַיִם, is used mainly for things that come in pairs, such as יָדַיִם, “hands”, and עֵינַיִם, “eyes”, and for two units of time: יוֹמַיִם, “two days” (Exodus 21:21); שְׁנָתַיִם, “two years” (Genesis 11:10). Some words only look dual or plural: שָׁמַיִם, “heavens”, and מַיִם, “water”. אֱלֹהִים, “God”, has a plural ending but takes a singular verb when it means the God of Israel: בָּרָא אֱלֹהִים, “God created” (Genesis 1:1).
Endings and gender do not always match. אָבוֹת, “fathers”, is masculine with ◌וֹת; נָשִׁים, “women”, is feminine with ◌ִים. Agreement follows the word’s gender, not its ending: הַמְּאֹרֹת הַגְּדֹלִים, “the great lights” (Genesis 1:16), has a masculine adjective.
A parsing marks a form **common** when it serves both genders, as many verb forms and pronouns do: אֲנִי, “I”, is the same for a man or a woman.

= hebrew-construct	The Construct State	hebrew
English says “the word of the LORD”. Hebrew puts the two nouns side by side, the first bound to the second: דְּבַר־יְהוָה (Genesis 15:1). The first noun is in the **construct** state, and “of” is understood after it. A noun’s ordinary form is called the **absolute**.
A construct noun is said together with the next word and loses its own stress, so its vowels often shorten, and some endings change:
| Absolute | Construct | Example |
| דָּבָר, word | דְּבַר | דְּבַר־יְהוָה, the word of the LORD |
| דְּבָרִים, words | דִּבְרֵי | דִּבְרֵי הַבְּרִית, the words of the covenant |
| תּוֹרָה, law | תּוֹרַת | תּוֹרַת יְהוָה, the law of the LORD |
| בַּיִת, house | בֵּית | מִבֵּית עֲבָדִים, out of the house of slavery |
The examples are from Genesis 15:1, Exodus 34:28, Psalm 19:7 and Exodus 20:2.
A chain is definite or indefinite as a whole, and only its last noun can take the article. If the last noun is definite, by the article, a suffix or being a name, the whole chain is: דִּבְרֵי הַבְּרִית is “the words of the covenant”, though דִּבְרֵי has no article. A construct noun never has the article itself.
Chains can be longer than two: מִכֹּל עֵץ־הַגָּן, “from every tree of the garden” (Genesis 2:16). An adjective describing the first noun comes after the whole chain.

= hebrew-suffixes	Pronoun Suffixes	hebrew
Hebrew adds “my”, “your”, “his” and the like as endings, called **pronominal suffixes**. On a noun they show whose it is: רֹעִי, “my shepherd” (Psalm 23:1); בְּצַלְמוֹ, “in his image” (Genesis 1:27).
| Person | Suffix | On סוּס, horse |
| my | ◌ִי | סוּסִי |
| your (m.) | ◌ְךָ | סוּסְךָ |
| your (f.) | ◌ֵךְ | סוּסֵךְ |
| his | ◌וֹ | סוּסוֹ |
| her | ◌ָהּ | סוּסָהּ |
| our | ◌ֵנוּ | סוּסֵנוּ |
| your (m. pl.) | ◌ְכֶם | סוּסְכֶם |
| your (f. pl.) | ◌ְכֶן | סוּסְכֶן |
| their (m.) | ◌ָם | סוּסָם |
| their (f.) | ◌ָן | סוּסָן |
Hebrew has different words for “your” spoken to a man and to a woman: Ruth says to Naomi עַמֵּךְ עַמִּי, “your people will be my people” (Ruth 1:16).
On a plural noun the suffixes come after a י, a sign of the plural: פָּנָיו, “his face” (Numbers 6:25, a noun that is always plural); עֵינֵיכֶם, “your eyes” (Genesis 3:5). Some prepositions take this plural set too: עָלָיו, “over him”; אֵלֶיךָ, “to you”.
On prepositions they mean “me”, “you”, “him” and so on: לְךָ, “to you” (Numbers 6:26); לוֹ, “to him” (Genesis 3:9). On the object marker they make object pronouns: אֹתוֹ, “him”; אֹתָם, “them” (Genesis 1:27). On verbs they are the object, and “me” is ◌נִי: יְבָרֶכְךָ יְהוָה וְיִשְׁמְרֶךָ, “The LORD bless you and keep you” (Numbers 6:24); שָׁמְרֵנִי, “keep me” (Psalm 16:1).
A noun often shortens its vowels before a suffix, as דָּבָר does in דְּבָרוֹ, “his word”, so look for the suffix at the end first.

= hebrew-pronouns	Personal Pronouns and Demonstratives	hebrew
The personal pronouns stand as words of their own. They are used mostly as subjects, often in sentences without a verb: אֲנִי יְהוָה, “I am the LORD” (Exodus 6:2). With a verb, whose form already shows its subject, they add emphasis or contrast.
| Person | Singular | Plural |
| 1st | אֲנִי, אָנֹכִי, I | אֲנַחְנוּ, we |
| 2nd masculine | אַתָּה, you | אַתֶּם, you |
| 2nd feminine | אַתְּ, you | אַתֵּן, you |
| 3rd masculine | הוּא, he | הֵם, הֵמָּה, they |
| 3rd feminine | הִיא, she | הֵן, הֵנָּה, they |
אָנֹכִי is another form of “I”: אָנֹכִי יְהוָה אֱלֹהֶיךָ, “I am the LORD your God” (Exodus 20:2). In the five books of Moses, הִיא is often written הִוא but read as hi.
The **demonstratives** are זֶה, “this” (masculine), זֹאת, “this” (feminine), and אֵלֶּה, “these”. Like adjectives, they follow their noun and take the article when it does: הַדָּבָר הַזֶּה, “this thing” (Genesis 22:16); הַדְּבָרִים הָאֵלֶּה, “these things” (Genesis 15:1). For “that” and “those”, Hebrew uses the third person pronouns with the article: בַּיּוֹם הַהוּא, “on that day” (Judges 20:15). On their own they are pronouns: זֶה אֶל־זֶה, “one to another” (Isaiah 6:3).

= hebrew-asher	The Relative אֲשֶׁר	hebrew
אֲשֶׁר means **who, which** or **that**, and joins a description to a noun: הָאִישׁ אֲשֶׁר לֹא הָלַךְ, “the man who does not walk” (Psalm 1:1). Unlike English “who” and “whom”, it never changes its form.
Because it does not change, Hebrew often adds a pronoun later in the clause to show its part in it, which English leaves out: הָאָרֶץ אֲשֶׁר אַתָּה שֹׁכֵב עָלֶיהָ, “the land on which you are lying”, literally “the land which you are lying on it” (Genesis 28:13). Read to the end of the clause before deciding how אֲשֶׁר fits.
It can also stand without a noun before it, meaning “what” or “whoever”: אֶת־כָּל־אֲשֶׁר עָשָׂה, “all that he had made” (Genesis 1:31). With prepositions it makes words such as כַּאֲשֶׁר, “as, when”: כַּאֲשֶׁר קָרַב, “when he came near” (Exodus 32:19).
A short form, שֶׁ joined to the next word, is found in some later books, such as Ecclesiastes and the Song of Songs.
Do not confuse אֲשֶׁר with כִּי, “that, because, for”: כִּי־טוֹב, “that it was good” (Genesis 1:4).

= hebrew-object-marker	The Object Marker אֵת	hebrew
אֵת, often written אֶת־ with a maqqef, marks the **definite direct object** of a verb: the thing or person acted on, when it is definite. English has no word for it, and it is not translated: בָּרָא אֱלֹהִים אֵת הַשָּׁמַיִם וְאֵת הָאָרֶץ, “God created the heavens and the earth” (Genesis 1:1).
It is a useful signpost: where the word order leaves you unsure, אֵת shows which noun is the object. An indefinite object, “a man”, normally goes without it, and poetry often leaves it out.
With pronoun suffixes it becomes אֹת־ or אֶתְ־ and makes the words for “me”, “him”, “them” and so on: בָּרָא אֹתוֹ … בָּרָא אֹתָם, “he created him … he created them” (Genesis 1:27).
Do not confuse it with the preposition אֵת, “with”, which looks the same on its own but takes suffixes differently: אִתּוֹ, “with him” (Genesis 45:1), but אֹתוֹ, “him”. The lexicon lists them as two words.

= hebrew-adjectives	Adjectives	hebrew
A Hebrew adjective agrees with its noun in gender and number, and it is used in two ways.
An **attributive** adjective describes its noun, as in “the great light”. It comes after the noun and matches it in definiteness as well: if the noun has the article, so does the adjective. אֶת־הַמָּאוֹר הַגָּדֹל … וְאֶת־הַמָּאוֹר הַקָּטֹן, “the great light … and the small light” (Genesis 1:16). Read it as “the light, the great one”.
A **predicate** adjective says something about its noun, as in “the light is great”. It does not take the article, and it often comes first; English supplies “is”: טוֹב הַדָּבָר, “the word is good” (1 Kings 2:38); טוֹב־יְהוָה לַכֹּל, “The LORD is good to all” (Psalm 145:9).
So: a noun with the article, followed by an adjective with the article, means “the good …”; an adjective without the article, beside a noun with it, means “… is good”.
Adjectives take ◌ִים and ◌וֹת in the plural, and follow the noun’s gender even when its ending looks otherwise: הַמְּאֹרֹת הַגְּדֹלִים, “the great lights” (Genesis 1:16). An adjective can also stand for a noun, and with מִן it compares: “better than”.

= hebrew-verbs	Verbs: An Overview	hebrew
A Hebrew verb is built on a root, usually of three consonants, and tells several things in one word. Its **stem** (binyan) shows the kind of action: simple, passive, causative and so on. Its **conjugation** shows how the action is presented: perfect, imperfect, imperative, participle and others. And it shows **person, gender and number**: Hebrew tells “you” said to a man from “you” said to a woman, and in the plural “they” (masculine) from “they” (feminine).
A parsing names them in that order: וַיֹּאמֶר, “and he said”, is Qal, consecutive imperfect, third person masculine singular, of אָמַר.
| Conjugation | Qal of קָטַל, kill | Usual sense |
| Perfect | קָטַל | he killed |
| Imperfect | יִקְטֹל | he will kill, he kills |
| Consecutive imperfect | וַיִּקְטֹל | and he killed |
| Consecutive perfect | וְקָטַל | and he will kill |
| Imperative | קְטֹל | kill (a command) |
| Infinitive construct | קְטֹל | to kill, killing |
| Infinitive absolute | קָטוֹל | killing; surely |
| Participle | קֹטֵל | killing, one who kills |
Hebrew conjugations are less tied to time than English tenses. The perfect usually presents an action as complete and the imperfect as not complete; the time comes largely from the setting and from how the verbs are joined with וְ. In narrative the past is told with וַיִּקְטֹל forms; in instructions and prophecy the future is often carried on with וְקָטַל forms.
The person is shown at the end of the perfect and at the front of the imperfect. Learn those two sets of markers, and most verbs can be read.

= hebrew-perfect	The Perfect (Qatal)	hebrew
The **perfect**, also called **qatal** after its form קָטַל, presents an action as **complete**, seen as a whole. In stories and speech it is most often translated with an English past: בָּרָא אֱלֹהִים, “God created” (Genesis 1:1).
It is formed with **endings** on the root. The third person masculine singular has none, and lexicons list the verb under that form.
| Person | Singular | Plural |
| 3rd masculine | קָטַל, he killed | קָטְלוּ, they killed |
| 3rd feminine | קָטְלָה, she killed | (as masculine) |
| 2nd masculine | קָטַלְתָּ, you killed | קְטַלְתֶּם, you killed |
| 2nd feminine | קָטַלְתְּ, you killed | קְטַלְתֶּן, you killed |
| 1st common | קָטַלְתִּי, I killed | קָטַלְנוּ, we killed |
With verbs of state or of knowing it often has a present meaning: כִּי עַתָּה יָדַעְתִּי, “for now I know” (Genesis 22:12); לֹא יָדַעְתִּי, “I do not know” (Genesis 4:9). It can also be an English perfect, “I have done”, or pluperfect, “I had done”, as the setting needs: אֲשֶׁר עָשָׂה, “that he had made” (Genesis 1:31).
The endings are worth learning well: on a perfect, ◌תִּי is always “I”, ◌נוּ “we”, and ◌תָּ or ◌תְּ “you”. After וְ, a perfect often carries a sequence on into the future.

= hebrew-imperfect	The Imperfect (Yiqtol)	hebrew
The **imperfect**, also called **yiqtol** after its form יִקְטֹל, presents an action as **not complete**: in the future, repeated or habitual, or possible. English may need “will”, “would”, “may”, “can”, “used to” or a simple present: לֹא אֶחְסָר, “I shall not want” (Psalm 23:1).
It is formed mainly with **prefixes**: י for “he” and “they” (masculine), ת for “you” and “she”, א for “I” and נ for “we”, with some endings as well.
| Person | Singular | Plural |
| 3rd masculine | יִקְטֹל, he will kill | יִקְטְלוּ, they will kill |
| 3rd feminine | תִּקְטֹל, she will kill | תִּקְטֹלְנָה, they will kill |
| 2nd masculine | תִּקְטֹל, you will kill | תִּקְטְלוּ, you will kill |
| 2nd feminine | תִּקְטְלִי, you will kill | תִּקְטֹלְנָה, you will kill |
| 1st common | אֶקְטֹל, I will kill | נִקְטֹל, we will kill |
תִּקְטֹל is both “you” (to a man) and “she”; the setting shows which.
With לֹא it gives a lasting prohibition: לֹא תִּרְצָח, “You shall not murder” (Exodus 20:13).
Some forms in ◌וּ add an extra נ: יִקְטְלוּן for יִקְטְלוּ. This **paragogic nun** adds weight or sound but does not change the meaning.
After וַ with a dagesh, the same form is the consecutive imperfect, which tells past events. After a plain וְ it is a **conjunctive imperfect**, which keeps its own sense, often a purpose or a wish: “so that he may”, “and let him”.

= hebrew-wayyiqtol	The Consecutive Imperfect (Wayyiqtol)	hebrew
Hebrew stories are told in a chain of verbs that each begin with **וַ** and a dagesh in the next letter: וַיֹּאמֶר אֱלֹהִים יְהִי אוֹר וַיְהִי־אוֹר, “And God said, ‘Let there be light’, and there was light” (Genesis 1:3). This is the **consecutive imperfect**, also called **wayyiqtol** or the waw-consecutive.
It looks like an imperfect with וַ in front, but it tells **past** events, each usually following the one before. It is the main verb of Hebrew narrative: Genesis 1 moves on with וַיֹּאמֶר, “and he said”, וַיַּרְא, “and he saw”, וַיַּבְדֵּל, “and he separated”, and וַיִּקְרָא, “and he called”.
To recognise it, look for וַ, a dagesh, and the prefix of the imperfect: וַיִּ, וַתִּ, וַנִּ. The dagesh is often left out of a י with sheva, as in וַיְהִי. Before א it cannot stand, so the form is וָא: וָאֶשְׁמַע … וָאֹמַר, “Then I heard … and I said” (Isaiah 6:8). Many verbs are also shorter here: יִהְיֶה becomes וַיְהִי, and יִרְאֶה becomes וַיַּרְא.
Translations give the וַ as “and”, “then” or “so”, or leave it out. וַיְהִי, “and it came to pass”, often just marks a new step in the story.
Not every imperfect after a waw is consecutive. With a plain וְ and no dagesh it is a conjunctive imperfect, which keeps the imperfect’s own sense.

= hebrew-weqatal	The Consecutive Perfect (Weqatal)	hebrew
A perfect joined to **וְ** often carries on a sequence, usually in the future or as an instruction, after an imperfect, an imperative or a participle. This is the **consecutive perfect**, also called **weqatal**: וְאָהַבְתָּ אֵת יְהוָה אֱלֹהֶיךָ, “And you shall love the LORD your God” (Deuteronomy 6:5).
It is the mirror of the consecutive imperfect: a form that alone usually speaks of the past is used, after וְ, for what is still to come. Laws, instructions and prophecies run on long chains of them: וְנִפְקְחוּ עֵינֵיכֶם וִהְיִיתֶם כֵּאלֹהִים, “your eyes will be opened, and you will be like God” (Genesis 3:5).
Its form does not change, except that in “I” and “you” (singular) the stress often moves to the last syllable: וְקָטַלְתָּ, “and you shall kill”, is stressed on -tá.
Not every perfect after וְ carries the sequence on; in a story of the past it may simply mean “and he did”. The parsings call some of these forms conjunctive perfects rather than consecutive ones. Read either kind by the setting: after a future or a command, read the future; in a story of the past, read the past.

= hebrew-imperative	The Imperative	hebrew
The **imperative** gives a command or makes a request, and is used only for “you”. It is like the imperfect without its prefix: from תִּשְׁמַע, “you will hear”, comes the command שְׁמַע, “hear”: שְׁמַע יִשְׂרָאֵל, “Hear, O Israel” (Deuteronomy 6:4).
| Person | Qal of קָטַל |
| 2nd masculine singular | קְטֹל |
| 2nd feminine singular | קִטְלִי |
| 2nd masculine plural | קִטְלוּ |
| 2nd feminine plural | קְטֹלְנָה |
A longer form ending in ◌ָה is common and means the same. The word נָא often follows, softening the request: “please”. Suffixes add the object: שָׁמְרֵנִי, “keep me” (Psalm 16:1); שְׁלָחֵנִי, “send me” (Isaiah 6:8). Commands often come in a row: פְּרוּ וּרְבוּ, “Be fruitful and multiply” (Genesis 1:22).
In the other stems the imperative follows the stem’s pattern: דַּבֵּר, “speak” (Piel, 1 Samuel 3:10); הוֹצִיאוּ, “send out” (Hiphil, Genesis 45:1).
Hebrew never uses the imperative to forbid. “Do not” is אַל with the jussive, or לֹא with the imperfect.

= hebrew-cohortative-jussive	The Cohortative and Jussive	hebrew
Hebrew has two more forms for wishes and resolves, both built on the imperfect: one for the speaker and one for others.
The **cohortative** is for “I” and “we”: “let me”, “let us”, or a resolve, “I will”. It usually adds ◌ָה to the imperfect: נִלְבְּנָה לְבֵנִים, “let us make bricks” (Genesis 11:3); הָבָה נֵרְדָה וְנָבְלָה, “Come, let us go down and confuse” (Genesis 11:7). Verbs whose root ends in ה cannot add it, so their form is the same as the imperfect, and only the setting shows the sense: נַעֲשֶׂה אָדָם, “Let us make man” (Genesis 1:26). A parsing may call such a form a cohortative “if contextual”.
The **jussive** is for “he”, “she” and “they”, and sometimes “you”: “let him”, “may it”. For most verbs it looks just like the imperfect, but some verbs have a shorter form: יְהִי אוֹר, “Let there be light” (Genesis 1:3), where the imperfect would be יִהְיֶה. Blessings are often jussive: יְבָרֶכְךָ יְהוָה, “The LORD bless you” (Numbers 6:24).
With אַל the jussive forbids: אַל־תִּירָא, “Do not be afraid” (Genesis 15:1); אַל־תִּשְׁלַח יָדְךָ … וְאַל־תַּעַשׂ לוֹ מְאוּמָּה, “Do not lay your hand … or do anything to him” (Genesis 22:12), where תַּעַשׂ is the short form of תַּעֲשֶׂה.

= hebrew-infinitive-construct	The Infinitive Construct	hebrew
The **infinitive construct** is the verb used as a noun: “to keep”, “keeping”. In the Qal it usually has the same form as the masculine singular imperative, קְטֹל; in the other stems it follows the stem’s pattern, as the Piel לְקַדְּשׁוֹ, “to keep it holy” (Exodus 20:8).
Most often it follows a preposition. With לְ it gives a purpose or completes another verb, like English “to”: לִרְאוֹת אֶת־שָׁאוּל, “to see Saul” (1 Samuel 15:35). לֵאמֹר, literally “to say”, introduces quoted words after a verb of speaking, and is often left untranslated or given as “saying” (Genesis 1:22).
With בְּ or כְּ it makes a clause of time, “when”, and a suffix or a following noun gives its subject: בְּהִבָּרְאָם, “when they were created” (Genesis 2:4); בִּהְיוֹתָם בַּשָּׂדֶה, “while they were in the field” (Genesis 4:8); בְּיוֹם אֲכָלְךָ, “on the day you eat” (Genesis 2:17).
The infinitive construct does not show person or time; a suffix and the setting supply them. It is negated with לְבִלְתִּי: לְבִלְתִּי אֲכָל־מִמֶּנּוּ, “not to eat from it” (Genesis 3:11).

= hebrew-infinitive-absolute	The Infinitive Absolute	hebrew
The **infinitive absolute** is a bare form of the verb that never changes, in the Qal usually קָטוֹל or קָטֹל. It has two main uses.
Most often it stands just before a verb of the same root, to strengthen it. English uses “surely”, “indeed” or “freely”: מוֹת תָּמוּת, “you will surely die” (Genesis 2:17); אָכֹל תֹּאכֵל, “you may freely eat” (Genesis 2:16). The root is said twice, and the doubling gives the weight; translate the sense rather than the words. The two need not be in the same stem: מוֹת יוּמָת, “he shall surely be put to death” (Exodus 21:12).
It can also stand in place of another verb form, especially as a command: זָכוֹר אֶת־יוֹם הַשַּׁבָּת לְקַדְּשׁוֹ, “Remember the Sabbath day, to keep it holy” (Exodus 20:8).
When two forms of one root stand side by side, think of the infinitive absolute.

= hebrew-participle	The Participle	hebrew
The **participle** is a verb that works like an adjective or a noun. It has gender and number but not person, and it shows an action as **going on**, at whatever time the setting gives: past, present or future.
| | Masculine | Feminine |
| Singular | קֹטֵל | קֹטֶלֶת, קֹטְלָה |
| Plural | קֹטְלִים | קֹטְלוֹת |
As a verb it describes what is happening: דַּבֵּר כִּי שֹׁמֵעַ עַבְדֶּךָ, “Speak, for your servant is listening” (1 Samuel 3:10); וְרוּחַ אֱלֹהִים מְרַחֶפֶת, “and the Spirit of God was hovering” (Genesis 1:2). Such a sentence has no verb “to be”; the participle carries the action. After הִנֵּה it often shows that something is about to happen.
As a noun it names the one who does the action: שׁוֹמֵר יִשְׂרָאֵל, “he who keeps Israel” (Psalm 121:4); הֲשֹׁמֵר אָחִי אָנֹכִי, “Am I my brother’s keeper?” (Genesis 4:9).
The Qal also has a **passive participle**, קָטוּל, “killed”: בָּרוּךְ יְהוָה, “Blessed be the LORD” (Psalm 28:6). In most other stems the participle begins with מְ, מִ or מַ: מַבְדִּיל, “separating” (Hiphil, Genesis 1:6). The Niphal’s begins with נִ: נִצָּב, “standing” (Genesis 28:13).

= hebrew-stems	The Stems (Binyanim)	hebrew
A Hebrew root can be put into several patterns, called **stems** (in Hebrew **binyanim**, “buildings”). Each stem changes the kind of action: simple or strengthened, active or passive, causing or reflexive. The basic stem is the **Qal**; the others are named after the forms of the old model verb פָּעַל, “to do”: Niphal, Piel, Pual, Hithpael, Hiphil and Hophal.
| Stem | Usual sense | Perfect | Imperfect |
| Qal | simple action or state | קָטַל | יִקְטֹל |
| Niphal | passive or reflexive of the Qal | נִקְטַל | יִקָּטֵל |
| Piel | strengthened, or causing a state | קִטֵּל | יְקַטֵּל |
| Pual | passive of the Piel | קֻטַּל | יְקֻטַּל |
| Hithpael | reflexive or mutual | הִתְקַטֵּל | יִתְקַטֵּל |
| Hiphil | causing | הִקְטִיל | יַקְטִיל |
| Hophal | passive of the Hiphil | הָקְטַל | יָקְטַל |
Each stem has its marks: a prefix (נ, ה, הִת), a dagesh in the middle root letter, or a particular vowel. Learn the marks, and the stem can be recognised in any conjugation.
יָדַע, “to know”, is found in all seven. In the Qal it is “know”, in the Niphal “be known”, in the Hiphil “make known”, and in the Hithpael “make oneself known”: בְּהִתְוַדַּע יוֹסֵף, “when Joseph made himself known” (Genesis 45:1).
The senses in the table are tendencies, not rules. Few verbs appear in every stem, and a verb’s meaning in one stem cannot always be guessed from another, so the lexicon has the last word. The parsings spell some names Nifal, Hifil, Hofal and Hitpael; they are the same stems.

= hebrew-qal	The Qal	hebrew
The **Qal**, meaning “light” or “simple”, is the basic stem. Most verbs have it, and it is far more common than any other stem. It gives the plain meaning of the verb: שָׁמַר, “he kept”; אָמַר, “he said”; בָּרָא, “he created” (Genesis 1:1).
It has no prefix of its own in the perfect and no dagesh in the root. Its usual vowels are a and a in the perfect (קָטַל) and i and o in the imperfect (יִקְטֹל). Some verbs of state have other vowels in the perfect, as כָּבֵד, “be heavy”, and קָטֹן, “be small”; and many verbs with a guttural in the root have a in the imperfect, as יִשְׁמַע, “he will hear”.
Lexicons list a verb under its Qal perfect, third person masculine singular, even when that exact form is not found.
The Qal also has a **passive participle**, קָטוּל: בָּרוּךְ, “blessed”. And a few forms that look like the Pual or Hophal, from verbs that have no Piel or Hiphil to match, are taken by many grammars, and by these parsings, as an old **Qal passive**.

= hebrew-niphal	The Niphal	hebrew
The **Niphal** is most often the **passive** or **reflexive** of the Qal: “be done” or “do to oneself”. וְנִפְקְחוּ עֵינֵיכֶם, “your eyes will be opened” (Genesis 3:5); לְזֹאת יִקָּרֵא אִשָּׁה, “she shall be called woman” (Genesis 2:23).
To recognise it, look in the perfect and participle for a prefixed **נִ**: נִקְטַל, נִקְטָל. In the imperfect, imperative and infinitive the נ has merged into the first root letter, leaving a **dagesh** there after the prefix: יִקָּטֵל. So יִקָּרֵא is the Niphal of קָרָא, “call”. Both kinds stand side by side in וְנִפְקַדְתָּ כִּי יִפָּקֵד מוֹשָׁבֶךָ, “you will be missed, because your seat will be empty” (1 Samuel 20:18).
When the first root letter is a guttural, the vowels shift. The prefix of the perfect may be נֶ, as in נֶאֱמָנָה, “trustworthy” (Psalm 19:7); in the imperfect the guttural cannot be doubled, so the prefix vowel lengthens instead: יֵאָמֵן.
Some verbs have their basic meaning in the Niphal and are translated as active: נִשְׁבַּעְתִּי, “I have sworn” (Genesis 22:16).

= hebrew-piel	The Piel	hebrew
The **Piel** is recognised by a **dagesh in the middle root letter**: קִטֵּל, יְקַטֵּל. In the imperfect and participle the prefix has a sheva: יְ, מְ. When the middle letter is a guttural or ר, which cannot be doubled, the vowel before it may lengthen instead: בֵּרַךְ, “he blessed”; וַיְבָרֶךְ אֹתָם, “and he blessed them” (Genesis 1:22).
Its meaning depends on the verb. With some it strengthens or repeats the action: שָׁבַר is “break”, but the Piel is “shatter”: וַיְשַׁבֵּר אֹתָם, “and he smashed them” (Exodus 32:19). With others it brings about a state: קָדַשׁ is “be holy”, but the Piel is “make holy”: וַיְקַדֵּשׁ אֹתוֹ, “and he made it holy” (Genesis 2:3).
For many verbs the Piel is simply the usual form, with little or no Qal beside it: דִּבֶּר, “he spoke”; וַיְדַבֵּר יְהוָה אֶל־מֹשֶׁה, “And the LORD spoke to Moses” (Exodus 6:10).

= hebrew-pual	The Pual	hebrew
The **Pual** is the **passive** of the Piel: what the Piel does, done to the subject. The Piel of בָּרַךְ is “bless”, and the Pual is “be blessed”: יְהִי שֵׁם יְהוָה מְבֹרָךְ, “Blessed be the name of the LORD” (Psalm 113:2).
It keeps the Piel’s **dagesh in the middle root letter**, but has a **u vowel** (qibbuts) under the first: קֻטַּל, יְקֻטַּל, and the participle מְקֻטָּל. Before ר, which cannot be doubled, the u becomes o: מְבֹרָךְ.
The Pual is uncommon, and is most often met as a participle. The u vowel with a doubled middle letter is the sign to look for.

= hebrew-hithpael	The Hithpael	hebrew
The **Hithpael** (also spelled Hitpael) is usually **reflexive** or **mutual**: “do to oneself” or “do to one another”. It can also show an action done again and again, or back and forth. וַיִּתְחַבֵּא הָאָדָם, “and the man hid himself” (Genesis 3:8); וַיִּתְהַלֵּךְ חֲנוֹךְ אֶת־הָאֱלֹהִים, “And Enoch walked with God” (Genesis 5:24), from הָלַךְ, “go”.
To recognise it, look for a prefix with **ת**, הִתְ in the perfect, יִתְ in the imperfect and מִתְ in the participle, together with a dagesh in the middle root letter, as in the Piel: הִתְקַטֵּל. מִתְהַלֵּךְ בַּגָּן, “walking in the garden” (Genesis 3:8).
When the root begins with ס, צ, שׂ or שׁ, the ת changes places with that letter: from שָׁמַר comes וְיִשְׁתַּמֵּר (Micah 6:16), not יִתְשַׁמֵּר. Before ד, ט or ת the ת often merges with the letter.
The **Nithpael** is a rare stem much like it, with the prefix נִתְ, whose ת can merge with the next letter: וְנִכַּפֵּר לָהֶם הַדָּם, “and the bloodshed will be atoned for” (Deuteronomy 21:8).

= hebrew-hiphil	The Hiphil	hebrew
The **Hiphil** is usually **causative**: it makes someone or something do or be what the Qal describes. יָצָא, “go out”, becomes “bring out”: אֲשֶׁר הוֹצֵאתִיךָ מֵאֶרֶץ מִצְרַיִם, “who brought you out of the land of Egypt” (Exodus 20:2). מָלַךְ, “reign”, becomes “make king”: כִּי־הִמְלִיךְ אֶת־שָׁאוּל, “that he had made Saul king” (1 Samuel 15:35).
To recognise it, look for a prefixed **ה** in the perfect, imperative and infinitive (הִקְטִיל); an **a** vowel under the prefix of the imperfect and participle (יַקְטִיל, מַקְטִיל); and often a hireq-yod, ◌ִי, between the second and third root letters: מַבְדִּיל, “separating” (Genesis 1:6).
In short forms, such as the jussive and the consecutive imperfect, the ◌ִי often becomes ◌ֵ: וַיַּבְדֵּל, “and he separated” (Genesis 1:4).
Some Hiphils are simply the usual active form of a verb, without a causing sense to look for: מִי הִגִּיד לְךָ, “Who told you?” (Genesis 3:11).

= hebrew-hophal	The Hophal	hebrew
The **Hophal** is the **passive** of the Hiphil: “be made to do” or “be made to be”. The Hiphil of מָלַךְ is “make king”, and the Hophal is “be made king”: אֲשֶׁר הָמְלַךְ עַל מַלְכוּת כַּשְׂדִּים, “who was made king over the kingdom of the Chaldeans” (Daniel 9:1).
To recognise it, look for an **o** or **u** vowel under the prefix: הָ (a qamets hatuf, said ho) or הֻ in the perfect, and יָ or יֻ in the imperfect: הָקְטַל, יָקְטַל. In some weak verbs the vowel is וּ: מוֹת יוּמָת, “he shall surely be put to death” (Exodus 21:12).
The Hophal is one of the less common stems. An o or u vowel under the prefix is its sign.

= hebrew-weak-verbs	Weak Verbs	hebrew
Grammars teach the verb with a **strong** root, whose three letters never change. Many common verbs are **weak**: a root letter is a guttural, or a letter such as נ, ו, י or ה that may change or drop out. Their prefixes and endings are the same as any verb’s; what changes is the root. In brief:
**Gutturals** (א, ה, ח, ע, and often ר) cannot be doubled and prefer a vowels and composite shevas: יַעֲשֶׂה, “he will do” (1 Kings 2:38), rather than the i and sheva of יִקְטֹל.
**First-nun** verbs often lose their נ, leaving a dagesh in the next letter: נָתַן, “give”, has יִתֵּן, “he will give”, as in יִתֶּן־לְךָ, “May he give you” (Psalm 20:4).
**Hollow** verbs have ו or י as the middle letter, and it often vanishes. Lexicons list them by the infinitive, as קוּם, “arise”. The perfect is קָם, “he arose”, and the consecutive imperfect וַיָּקָם: וַיָּקָם קַיִן, “Cain rose up” (Genesis 4:8).
**Third-he** verbs end in ה in the lexicon: עָשָׂה, “do, make”; רָאָה, “see”; הָיָה, “be”. In short forms the ה and its vowel fall away: וַיַּעַשׂ, “and he made”; וַיַּרְא, “and he saw”; וַיְהִי, “and it was” (Genesis 1:3–7).
**First-yod** verbs often lose or change their י: יָשַׁב, “sit, dwell”, has וַיֵּשֶׁב, “and he stayed” (1 Kings 2:38).
When a form seems to have only two root letters, try each of these in turn, or open the word’s table of forms.

= hebrew-negation	Negation	hebrew
**לֹא** is the ordinary word for “not”. It negates statements with the perfect and imperfect: לֹא יָדַעְתִּי, “I do not know” (Genesis 4:9). With the imperfect it also gives a lasting prohibition, as in the commandments: לֹא תִּרְצָח, “You shall not murder” (Exodus 20:13).
**אַל** negates wishes and commands, with the jussive or cohortative. It is the usual way to say “do not” to someone in a particular moment: אַל־תִּירָא, “Do not be afraid” (Genesis 15:1).
**אֵין**, the construct of אַיִן, “nothing”, says “there is not”, or with a suffix “he is not”: אֵין אֱלֹהִים, “There is no God” (Psalm 14:1); וְאֵין עוֹד, “and there is no other” (Isaiah 45:5); וְאֵינֶנּוּ, “and he was no more” (Genesis 5:24). It is the opposite of יֵשׁ, “there is”, and often stands before a participle.
Other negatives include לְבִלְתִּי, used with the infinitive, and בְּלִי, “without”.
Hebrew does not put a negative with the imperative: a command and its prohibition use different forms.

= hebrew-questions	Questions	hebrew
Hebrew has no question mark. A question is shown by a question word, or by the prefix **הֲ** on its first word: הֲשֹׁמֵר אָחִי אָנֹכִי, “Am I my brother’s keeper?” (Genesis 4:9); הֲמִן־הָעֵץ … אָכָלְתָּ, “Have you eaten from the tree…?” (Genesis 3:11). Sometimes a question has no mark at all, and only the setting shows it.
Before a guttural the prefix is usually הַ, and then it can look exactly like the article; only the sense tells them apart: הַחֵפֶץ לַיהוָה, “Does the LORD delight…?” (1 Samuel 15:22). Elsewhere the article doubles the next letter with a dagesh, and the question prefix does not.
The common question words:
**מִי**, “who?”: מִי הִגִּיד לְךָ, “Who told you?” (Genesis 3:11); אֶת־מִי אֶשְׁלַח, “Whom shall I send?” (Isaiah 6:8).
**מָה**, “what?”, often joined by a maqqef and doubling the next letter: מַה־מָּתוֹק, “What is sweeter…?” (Judges 14:18). From it comes **לָמָּה**, “why?”, literally “for what?”.
**אַיֵּה** and **אֵי**, “where?”: אֵי הֶבֶל אָחִיךָ, “Where is your brother Abel?” (Genesis 4:9); with a suffix, אַיֶּכָּה, “Where are you?” (Genesis 3:9). And **אֵיךְ**, “how?”.

= hebrew-hinneh	The Word הִנֵּה	hebrew
**הִנֵּה** points to something and asks the hearer to look. Older translations give “behold”; newer ones “look”, “see”, “here is”, or no word at all.
In speech it calls attention to what is present or about to happen. With a suffix it says “here I am”: הִנֵּנִי, Abraham’s answer when God called him (Genesis 22:1), and Isaiah’s הִנְנִי שְׁלָחֵנִי, “Here am I. Send me” (Isaiah 6:8). Followed by a participle it often means that something is happening now or is about to.
In narrative, וְהִנֵּה often lets the reader see through a character’s eyes: וַיַּרְא אֱלֹהִים … וְהִנֵּה־טוֹב מְאֹד, “God saw … and indeed, it was very good” (Genesis 1:31); וְהִנֵּה יְהוָה נִצָּב עָלָיו, “and there the LORD stood” (Genesis 28:13).
It can also bring in a point in an argument: הִנֵּה שְׁמֹעַ מִזֶּבַח טוֹב, “To obey is better than sacrifice” (1 Samuel 15:22). The shorter הֵן works in much the same way.

= hebrew-word-order	Word Order	hebrew
In Hebrew narrative the verb usually comes **first**, then the subject, then the object: וַיַּרְא אֱלֹהִים אֶת־הָאוֹר, “And God saw the light” (Genesis 1:4). To find the subject, look first at the verb’s person, gender and number, then for a noun that matches; a definite object often has אֵת.
When a noun comes before the verb, the writer is doing something on purpose: giving background, marking a contrast, or starting something new. Such a clause often begins with וְ and the noun, and translations may say “now”: וְהָאָרֶץ הָיְתָה תֹהוּ וָבֹהוּ, “Now the earth was formless and void” (Genesis 1:2); וְהַנָּחָשׁ הָיָה עָרוּם, “Now the serpent was crafty” (Genesis 3:1); וְהָאֱלֹהִים נִסָּה אֶת־אַבְרָהָם, “God tested Abraham” (Genesis 22:1).
Many sentences have **no verb** at all, and English supplies “is” or “was”: יְהוָה רֹעִי, “The LORD is my shepherd” (Psalm 23:1); אֲנִי יְהוָה, “I am the LORD” (Exodus 6:2). These are called verbless or nominal clauses, and they describe or identify.
Within a phrase the order is fixed: a noun comes before its adjective, a construct noun before the noun it is bound to, and prefixes and suffixes are on the word itself.

= hebrew-aramaic	Aramaic in the Old Testament	hebrew
A few parts of the Old Testament are written not in Hebrew but in **Aramaic**, a related language that became the common language of the Near East: Ezra 4:8–6:18 and 7:12–26, Daniel 2:4–7:28, Jeremiah 10:11, and two words in Genesis 31:47. Daniel marks the change itself: the Chaldeans answer the king אֲרָמִית, “in Aramaic”, and their next words are Aramaic: מַלְכָּא לְעָלְמִין חֱיִי, “O king, live for ever” (Daniel 2:4).
Aramaic uses the same letters and points, and many words are the same or similar. A few differences show at once.
For “the”, Aramaic adds ◌ָא at the end of a noun instead of הַ at the front: מַלְכָּא, “the king”; חֶלְמָא, “the dream” (Daniel 2:4). A parsing calls this the **determinate** (or emphatic) state.
The word for “who, which, that”, and for “of” between nouns, is often דִּי.
The verb stems have other names, such as Peal for the simple stem, Pael for the doubled one, and Haphel or Aphel for the causative.
The lexicon marks Aramaic words as their own entries, so a word in these chapters may have an entry apart from a Hebrew word that looks the same.
