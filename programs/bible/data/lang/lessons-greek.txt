section	The Alphabet	Each lesson explains a few letters, asks their names and sounds, then has you write words in English letters.

= familiar	Six Familiar Letters
Greek has 24 letters. Many look like ours and sound as you would expect, so they are the place to start.
These six are the vowels α, ε, ι, ο and the consonants κ and τ. Each word in the exercises uses only the letters you have learned so far.
Greek words carry small marks over their letters, such as ἐ, ά and ῖ. Read past them for now; a later lesson explains them.
Some vowels go together as one sound: αι as in aisle, ει as in eight, οι as in oil. So καί, _and_, written _kai_, is said kye.
Once you have written a word, you see how it is said, with its stressed syllable in capitals: κατά is said kah-TAH.

= misleading	Letters That Mislead
These six look like letters of ours but are not those letters. Take a moment over each.
η looks like an n but is a long e, written _ē_. ν looks like a v but is n. ρ looks like a p but is r. υ looks like a u, and is written _y_ on its own. χ looks like an x but is written _ch_ in English letters, as in _Christos_, and said like the ch of Bach, a breathy k, written kh in the pronunciations; many people simply say k, as in Christ. ω looks like a w but is a long o, written _ō_.

= marks	Breathing Marks and Pairs
A word that begins with a vowel has a breathing mark over it. The smooth breathing, ἀ, is silent. The rough breathing, ἁ, is an h before the vowel, as in ἡμέρα, _hēmera_. A ρ at the start of a word takes it too: ῥῆμα, _rhēma_.
Some vowels go together as one sound, and υ is written _u_ in them: ου as in soup, αυ as in out, ευ as in feud.
γ before γ, κ or χ is said as n: ἄγγελος is _angelos_. The other marks over letters, the accents, show where a word is stressed; they do not change how it is written in Latin letters.

section	Words	The New Testament's most common words, a few at a time, each lesson then reading verses with them, beginning with John 1.

= in-the-beginning	In the Beginning
These lessons teach words, not grammar. About a hundred words make up two of every three in the New Testament, and with them you can follow a verse word by word.
A Greek word changes its ending as it is used: λόγος, _word_, is also λόγου, λόγῳ and λόγον. Each word is shown here as a dictionary gives it; in a verse, it may end differently.
The most common word, _the_, changes most of all: ὁ, ἡ, τό, τοῦ, τῷ, τόν, τήν and more are all the same word.
With these words you can read the first verses of John.
